Searching for tajweed classes Ottawa families can rely on? Most are offered through masjids and their Quran schools, weekend Islamic programs, and private or online teachers. The right class for your child is not the closest or the cheapest one, but the one where a qualified teacher hears your child recite every session and corrects them one-to-one. This guide lists verified local options and gives you a simple way to evaluate any tajweed class before you register.

We are a Quran and Arabic school in Nepean, so we have a view, but this guide is meant to help you choose well wherever your child ends up.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Several Ottawa masjids run children’s Quran programs that include tajweed, on weekday evenings and weekends, at a wide range of prices.
> - The most important number is how many minutes your child actually recites to the teacher each week.
> - Ask about the teacher’s training and chain of recitation, placement testing, class size and how progress is reported.
> - For children, tajweed is learned by listening and correction, not by memorizing rule names.
> - Always confirm schedules and fees directly with the program: they change each year.

## What a good tajweed class actually looks like

**Tajweed** means reciting each letter from its correct place, with its correct qualities, and applying the rules of ghunnah, madd, noon and meem sakinah and stopping. If you want a refresher on the rules themselves, our guide to [tajweed rules for kids](/blog/tajweed-rules-for-kids) explains them with examples from Juz Amma.

Tajweed has always been taught face to face: the student listens to the teacher, recites back, and the teacher corrects. This is sometimes called *talaqqi* (receiving directly from a teacher). A good class keeps that at the centre. In practice, it usually looks like this:

1. A short warm-up or review of a rule, often with the teacher modelling slowly.
2. Each child recites individually to the teacher while others practise quietly or listen.
3. The teacher corrects specific words, has the child repeat, and notes the next portion.
4. A written or digital record of what was recited and what needs work.

If most of the class is spent on worksheets, group chanting or videos, your child may be learning *about* tajweed rather than learning to recite with it.

## The minutes that matter: individual recitation time

The single best question to ask any tajweed program is: **how many minutes will my child recite to the teacher each week?**

You can estimate it yourself. Take the time set aside for individual recitation and divide by the number of children per teacher.

| Recitation time per session | Children per teacher | Approximate minutes per child |
|---|---|---|
| 60 minutes | 6 | 10 |
| 60 minutes | 10 | 6 |
| 60 minutes | 15 | 4 |
| 90 minutes | 8 | 11 |

Then multiply by sessions per week. A weekday program that meets four evenings a week with a small group can give a child far more correction time than a once-a-week class, even if the weekly class lasts longer. This is why weekday-evening masjid programs are popular for children who are serious about tajweed and hifz, and why weekend programs work best when combined with daily practice at home.

## How to evaluate a tajweed class: 10 questions

Use these on a phone call or visit. A good program will be happy to answer.

**1. Who teaches, and how were they trained?** Ask where the teacher learned tajweed and whether they hold an **ijazah** (a certification from a teacher with a chain of recitation) in Hafs ‘an ‘Asim. An ijazah is not the only sign of a good children’s teacher, but it tells you the teacher’s own recitation was checked carefully. Our explainer on [what an ijazah is](/blog/ijazah-explained) covers what to ask.

**2. Is there a placement assessment?** Children who cannot yet read fluently need a qaida-level class. Mixing them with fluent readers frustrates both.

**3. What is taught, and in what order?** Common paths are a primer such as the Noorani Qaida, then reading from the mushaf with rules introduced gradually. Older students may study classical texts like *Tuhfat al-Atfal*. See [Noorani Qaida vs Qaida Baghdadia](/blog/noorani-qaida-vs-qaida-baghdadia) for how primers differ.

**4. How many children per teacher?** Use the table above.

**5. How do teachers correct mistakes?** Look for specific, kind correction: the teacher models the word, the child repeats, and the teacher checks again. Avoid programs where children are shamed for mistakes.

**6. How is progress reported?** Ask whether you will see what your child recited each week and what to practise at home. Some programs use a notebook; others use an app or portal.

**7. Is memorization included, and how is it balanced with tajweed?** Some programs focus on memorizing quickly. For tajweed, a child needs time to recite slowly and be corrected, so ask how the two are balanced.

**8. What are the safety and supervision policies?** Ask who supervises children during breaks, whether staff and volunteers have police checks, and how classrooms are arranged. In Ottawa, people in positions of trust with children can obtain a [Vulnerable Sector Check](https://www.ottawapolice.ca/en/reports-and-services/police-record-and-background-checks/police-record-checks/) from the Ottawa Police Service, so it is fair to ask whether teachers have one.

**9. What does it cost, and what is included?** Masjid programs are often modestly priced: Masjid Bilal, for example, posts $50 a month for its weekend children’s track and $75 for its weekday track. Broader programs that combine Quran with other subjects cost more. Compare cost per hour of instruction and per minute of individual recitation, not just the monthly fee.

**10. Can my child try a class first?** A trial session or an open-house visit shows you the atmosphere better than any website.

### Red flags

- No one can tell you how the teacher was trained.
- Your child rarely recites individually.
- Rule names are drilled but recitation is not corrected.
- No communication with parents about progress.
- Harsh discipline or a fearful atmosphere around the Quran.

## Tajweed classes for children by age

**Ages 4–6:** Look for a warm, play-friendly class where children copy the teacher’s sounds and learn short surahs. Terminology is not needed. In QAF’s [Islamic kindergarten in Ottawa](/program), for example, JK and SK children learn short surahs from Juz Amma by listening and repeating every school day.

**Ages 7–11:** This is the ideal window for formal tajweed. Children can read, enjoy spotting rules with colours, and can hold their mouth positions consciously. Weekly correction plus daily home practice works well.

**Ages 12–15:** Teens can study the rules in depth and often benefit from a teacher who explains *why*, not just *what*. Some are ready to work toward reciting a full juz with excellent tajweed. Our guide to [Quran classes for teens in Ottawa](/blog/quran-classes-for-teens-ottawa) looks at options for this age group.

**Parents:** Several Ottawa masjids list brothers’ and sisters’ Quran classes for ages 15 or 16 and up. Learning alongside your child, even at a different level, is one of the strongest motivators you can give them.

## Making tajweed classes work at home

A weekly class gives correction. Home practice makes it stick.

- Practise five days a week for 10–15 minutes, ideally at the same time.
- Play a slow, clear reciter and have your child repeat each phrase.
- Ask your child to teach you the rule they learned that week.
- Keep the teacher’s notes in one place and review them before the next class.
- Encourage effort. The Prophet ﷺ said the one who recites the Quran with difficulty, stumbling over it, has two rewards ([Sahih Muslim 798a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:798a)).
