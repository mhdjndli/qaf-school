Choosing between an Islamic school vs public school in Ontario comes down to four things: what your child learns each day, who teaches them, what it costs, and how much of your family's faith and language the school carries for you. Public and Catholic board schools are free and regulated by the province. Islamic schools are private, charge tuition, and build Quran, Arabic and Islamic manners into the school day. Neither is automatically "better". The right choice depends on your child, your budget and your plan for the years after kindergarten.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Ontario public and Catholic schools are free and must follow the Ontario curriculum. Islamic schools are private, receive no provincial funding, and set their own tuition.
> - Teachers in public and Catholic schools must be certified by the Ontario College of Teachers (OCT). Private school teachers are not required to be, so it is worth asking.
> - Public schools must accommodate religious practice (Eid absences, prayer, dress) under Ontario human rights law, but faith is not taught as part of the day.
> - Islamic schools offer daily Quran, Arabic and Islamic Studies, a calendar that respects Eid, and an environment where Muslim practice is the norm.
> - Many Ontario families mix both: an Islamic kindergarten followed by public school, or public school plus a weekend Islamic program.

## Islamic school vs public school at a glance

Ontario has three main kinds of elementary schooling open to most Muslim families: public (secular) board schools, Catholic board schools, and private schools, which include Islamic schools. French-language public and Catholic boards also exist for eligible families; we compare all four Ottawa boards in our guide to [Ottawa school boards compared](/blog/ottawa-school-boards-compared).

| | Public board school | Catholic board school | Islamic (private) school |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cost** | Free | Free | Tuition (varies by school) |
| **Funding** | Provincially funded | Provincially funded | No government funding |
| **Curriculum** | Ontario curriculum (required) | Ontario curriculum plus Catholic religious education | Not required to follow the Ontario curriculum; many choose to align with it |

The table simplifies a lot. Individual schools vary, and the details below matter more than the summary.

## Curriculum: what your child actually learns

Public and Catholic board schools must teach the Ontario curriculum. For four- and five-year-olds, that means The Kindergarten Program, which is organized around four "frames": belonging and contributing, self-regulation and well-being, demonstrating literacy and mathematics behaviours, and problem solving and innovating. Learning is play-based and inquiry-based.

Private schools in Ontario are treated differently. According to the [Government of Ontario's private schools page](https://www.ontario.ca/page/private-schools), private elementary schools are not inspected by the Ministry of Education and do not need to follow the official Ontario curriculum, although they should offer general subjects such as math, science, language and social studies.

In practice, most established Islamic schools in Ontario follow or align with the provincial curriculum and add religious and language subjects on top. That is the question to ask each school directly: "Do you follow the Ontario curriculum, and how do you fit Quran, Arabic and Islamic Studies around it?"

### What the extra subjects look like

In an Islamic kindergarten, the additions are usually:

- **Quran**: short surahs, often from Juz Amma, learned by listening and repetition.
- **Arabic**: letters, sounds, vocabulary and simple conversation, ideally through daily immersion rather than a weekly class.
- **Islamic Studies and adab**: duas for daily moments, manners, stories of the prophets, and basic acts of worship.

## Teachers and certification

This is one of the most important and least-discussed differences.

Every teacher in an Ontario public or Catholic board school must be a member in good standing of the Ontario College of Teachers. The provincial private schools page states plainly that principals and teachers in private schools do not need to be OCT-certified.

That does not mean private school teachers are less skilled. Many Islamic school teachers hold OCT certification, Early Childhood Education diplomas, Montessori training, or Islamic and Arabic qualifications that public schools do not require at all. But it does mean the burden of checking is on you. Ask:

- How many of your classroom teachers are OCT-certified or Registered Early Childhood Educators?
- Who teaches Quran and Arabic, and what are their qualifications?
- Do all staff have current criminal reference checks?

You can look up any Ontario teacher's certification yourself on the Ontario College of Teachers public register.

### The kindergarten team

In public and Catholic full-day kindergarten, classes with more than 16 children are staffed by a teacher and a designated early childhood educator working together, according to [Ontario's kindergarten page](https://www.ontario.ca/page/kindergarten). Ask any Islamic school how its classrooms are staffed and what the adult-to-child ratio is.

## Cost: free versus tuition

Public and Catholic kindergarten is free. Islamic schools in Ontario receive no provincial funding, so tuition pays for teachers, rent and materials.

Tuition varies widely between schools, and the sticker price is only part of the picture. Compare registration fees, resource or book fees, uniform costs, whether before- and after-school care is included, and whether the program runs 10 or 12 months. For reference, QAF's kindergarten tuition is $690 per month for 10 months (September to June) plus a $190 annual resource fee, for a total of $7,090 per year, with a sibling discount. We break down how Ottawa fees compare in [Ottawa Islamic school fees explained](/blog/ottawa-islamic-school-fees-explained).

A few cost points families often miss:

- **Transportation.** Board schools provide yellow-bus service to eligible students. Most private schools do not, so factor in daily driving time.
- **Before- and after-school care.** Board schools usually host extended-day programs on site, which are fee-based and may be eligible for the City's child care fee subsidy.
- **Taxes.** Private elementary tuition is generally not tax-deductible. Some religious schools that are registered charities can issue donation receipts for the portion of fees tied to religious instruction, under the Canada Revenue Agency's guidance in [IC75-23](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/publications/ic75-23.html). Ask each school what, if anything, it can receipt, and check with a tax professional.

## Class size and attention

Ontario's [class size regulation (O. Reg. 132/12)](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/regulation/120132) requires boards to keep the average size of kindergarten classes at 26, with individual kindergarten classes generally capped at 29. In Grades 1 to 3, most classes must have 20 or fewer students, with a hard limit of 23.

Private schools set their own class sizes. Many Islamic schools run smaller classes, but not all, and a small class with one adult is not necessarily better than a larger class with a teacher and an ECE. Ask about both the number of children and the number of adults in the room.

## Religious accommodation in public schools

If you choose a public school, your child does not have to leave their faith at the door. Ontario's Education Act allows students to be excused for a day regarded as a holy day by their religion, and the [Ontario Human Rights Commission's policy on creed](https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/policy-preventing-discrimination-based-creed) requires organizations to accommodate sincerely held religious practices to the point of undue hardship. The Ottawa-Carleton District School Board publishes its own religious accommodation guidelines.

In practice, families commonly request:

- **Eid absences** without academic penalty.
- **A quiet space for prayer** for older students, especially for Dhuhr in winter.
- **Accommodation during Ramadan** for older students who fast, such as somewhere other than the lunchroom.
- **Modest dress** for physical education and swimming.
- **Advance notice** about celebrations, music, or food activities so you can discuss alternatives.

The practical tip: put requests in writing to the principal early, before Ramadan or Eid, and keep the tone collaborative. Most schools respond well.

What accommodation cannot do is teach. A public school will respect your child's Islam, but it will not teach Quran, Arabic, salah or Islamic manners. That part falls to your family, your masjid, or a [weekend Islamic school](/blog/weekend-islamic-schools-ottawa).

## Catholic schools for Muslim families

Ontario's Catholic boards are publicly funded, free, and follow the Ontario curriculum with Catholic religious education added. Some Muslim families choose them because faith is taken seriously in the building, and staff are used to discussing God, prayer and values.

Admission rules for non-Catholic children vary by board, so ask before you plan around it. In Ottawa, the Ottawa Catholic School Board says on its [kindergarten registration article](https://www.ocsb.ca/?p=58182) that families of all faiths are welcome. Expect Catholic prayer and religion classes to be part of the day, and ask the school how it handles participation for a Muslim child.

## Identity, belonging and diversity

For many parents, this is the real question. The early years are when children form their sense of who they are and where they belong.

In an Islamic school, Muslim practice is simply normal. Saying "bismillah" before eating, wearing hijab, fasting, or leaving for Jumu'ah needs no explanation. Children see their teachers pray, hear Arabic all day, and celebrate Eid with classmates. For a young child, that kind of everyday normality is powerful. We explore this further in [building Islamic identity in the early years](/blog/building-islamic-identity-in-early-years).

Public schools offer something different and also valuable: daily friendship with children of many faiths, cultures and languages. Ottawa's schools are highly diverse, and many Muslim children thrive there with strong support at home.

Islamic schools are also more diverse than people expect. A single classroom can include families from a dozen countries and several mother tongues, united by faith rather than ethnicity.

There is no wrong answer here. A child with a strong Islamic foundation at home can do well in public school, and a child in an Islamic school still needs to learn to live confidently in a diverse Canadian society.

## The transition to Grade 1

Many Islamic schools in Ontario, including QAF, are kindergarten-only or run to a certain grade. If you start in an Islamic kindergarten, plan the next step early.

Moving from a private kindergarten to a public or Catholic Grade 1 is straightforward. Children are entitled to attend their designated board school, and you register them like any other Grade 1 student. There is no entrance test. The key is readiness: a child who has had a strong kindergarten experience with play-based literacy, numeracy and self-regulation usually settles in quickly. Our guide to [preparing your child for Grade 1](/blog/preparing-your-child-for-grade-1) covers the skills that matter most.

Two tips for a smooth move:

- **Register early.** Board registration for the following September usually opens in the winter, so contact your designated school early in the new year.
- **Keep the faith learning going.** Once your child moves to a board school, a weekend or after-school Quran and Arabic program helps protect what they built in kindergarten. For children aged 8 to 15, QAF's [Sunday Camp](/sunday-camp) continues Quran, Arabic, math and STEM.

## How to decide: questions to ask yourself

There is no universal answer, so it helps to be honest about your priorities.

**An Islamic school may be the better fit if:**

- You want Quran, Arabic and Islamic manners taught every day, not just on weekends.
- You do not have the time or confidence to teach them consistently at home.
- Your child would benefit from an environment where their practice is the norm.
- The tuition fits comfortably within your budget.

**A public or Catholic school may be the better fit if:**

- Free tuition and bus service matter a lot for your family right now.
- You can commit to strong religious and Arabic learning at home and through a weekend program.
- You want certified-teacher guarantees and provincial oversight.
- The nearest Islamic school is too far for a daily commute.

Whatever you lean toward, visit. Sit in a classroom, ask about the curriculum and teacher qualifications, and watch how the adults speak to children. Our [guide to Islamic schools in Ottawa](/blog/islamic-schools-in-ottawa-guide) lists local options to compare side by side.

## Conclusion

The difference between an Islamic school and a public school in Ontario is not about academic quality alone. Public and Catholic schools offer free, regulated education with certified teachers and accommodation for your faith. Islamic schools offer daily Quran, Arabic and an Islamic environment, at a cost, with oversight that depends on each school's own standards. Many families use both at different stages.
