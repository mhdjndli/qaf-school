Makharij for kids means teaching children *where* each Arabic letter comes from: the throat, the tongue, the lips, the nose or the open space of the mouth. There are five main areas and, in the standard teaching followed by most Quran teachers, 17 exact articulation points for all the Arabic letters. This guide explains every area in plain words, with a table you can print, a simple “find the letter” test, the letter pairs children mix up most, and games that make practice fun.

Makharij is the first building block of tajweed. When a child says each letter from the right place, most of the clear mistakes in Quran recitation disappear.

> **Key takeaways**
> - A **makhraj** (plural *makharij*) is the exact place in the mouth or throat where a letter’s sound is made.
> - There are five main areas: the empty space (jawf), throat (halq), tongue (lisan), lips (shafatan) and nasal passage (khayshum).
> - To find a letter’s makhraj, say it with a sukun after a hamza with a vowel, like “أَبْ” or “أَقْ,” and notice where the sound stops.
> - English-speaking children usually struggle with ح/ه, ع/ء, ق/ك, ص/س, ض/د, ط/ت and ث ذ ظ.
> - Young children learn makharij best by copying a teacher and using mirrors, not by memorizing terms.

## What makharij means, and why it matters

**Makharij al-huruf** (مخارج الحروف) literally means “the exit points of the letters.” Each letter has its own place of articulation, and many Arabic letters that sound alike to English ears are made in different places.

This matters because changing the makhraj can change the word. In Surah Al-Ikhlas, the first word is “قُلْ” (*qul*, “say”), made deep in the mouth:

قُلْ هُوَ ٱللَّهُ أَحَدٌ

If a child says it with ك (*kul*), it becomes a different word that means “eat.” This is why teachers treat makharij mistakes as clear mistakes that must be corrected ([Surah Al-Ikhlas 112:1](https://quran.com/112/1)).

The effort is worth it. The Prophet ﷺ said that whoever recites a letter from Allah’s Book receives a good deed, and each good deed is multiplied ten times, adding: “I do not say that Alif Lam Mim is a letter, but Alif is a letter, Lam is a letter and Mim is a letter” ([Jami‘ at-Tirmidhi 2910](https://sunnah.com/tirmidhi:2910), graded hasan sahih gharib by at-Tirmidhi). Every letter counts, so it is worth saying each one well.

## The 5 main areas of makharij at a glance

| Area | Arabic name | Number of makharij | Letters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Empty space of the mouth and throat | الجوف (al-jawf) | 1 | The three madd letters: ا و ي when they stretch |
| Throat | الحلق (al-halq) | 3 | ء ه ع ح غ خ |
| Tongue | اللسان (al-lisan) | 10 | ق ك ج ش ي ض ل ن ر ط د ت ص ز س ظ ذ ث |
| Lips | الشفتان (ash-shafatan) | 2 | ف ب م و |
| Nasal passage | الخيشوم (al-khayshum) | 1 | The ghunnah sound of ن and م |

This is the system of Imam Ibn al-Jazari, the most widely taught approach in Quran classes using the Hafs ‘an ‘Asim recitation.

## How to find any letter’s makhraj: the “a-letter” test

Here is a classic teacher’s trick that children enjoy.

**Step 1:** Put a hamza with a fatha before the letter, and give the letter a sukun: أَبْ، أَتْ، أَقْ، أَحْ.

**Step 2:** Say it slowly and stop on the letter.

**Step 3:** Notice where the sound stops or where the air is squeezed. That spot is the makhraj.

Try “أَبْ” and your lips close. Try “أَتْ” and the tip of your tongue touches behind your top teeth. Try “أَقْ” and the back of your tongue rises to the back of the roof of your mouth. Children can feel the difference with a fingertip on their throat or by watching in a mirror.

## 1. The empty space: al-jawf

The **jawf** is the open space inside the mouth and throat. Three letters come from here, but only when they are **madd** (stretching) letters:

- **ا** alif after a fatha, as in “قَالَ”
- **و** waw with sukun after a damma, as in “يَقُولُ”
- **ي** ya with sukun after a kasra, as in “قِيلَ”

These sounds have no single contact point. The voice simply flows out until the breath ends, which is why they can be stretched. **Kid’s cue:** “Open the door and let the sound flow out.”

## 2. The throat: al-halq

The throat has **three** makharij, each with two letters. This is where English-speaking children need the most practice, because English has only one of these sounds (h).

| Part of the throat | Letters | Kid’s cue |
|---|---|---|
| Deepest part (closest to the chest) | ء ه | ء is a tiny “catch”; ه is a soft breath, like fogging a mirror |
| Middle | ع ح | ح is a squeezed, whispered breath, like the “haaa” after tasting something very spicy; ع is the same place with the voice on |
| Top (closest to the mouth) | غ خ | خ is like clearing your throat gently; غ is the same place with the voice on, like a gargle |

The classic pair to work on is **ح vs ه**. In Al-Fatiha, “ٱلْحَمْدُ” begins with ح from the middle of the throat:

ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ

Have your child say *al-hamd* with a soft ه and then with a squeezed ح, and feel the difference ([Surah Al-Fatiha 1:2](https://quran.com/1/2)). The same ayah also has ع in “ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ,” which should not become a plain “a.”

## 3. The tongue: al-lisan

The tongue has the most makharij: **10 points** for **18 letters**. Move from the back of the tongue to the tip.

| Part of the tongue | Touches or approaches | Letters | Kid’s cue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Very back | Soft back part of the roof of the mouth | ق | Deep “q,” like a drop of water |
| Back, a little forward of ق | Roof of the mouth | ك | Like the English “k” |
| Middle | Hard middle of the roof of the mouth | ج ش ي | ي here is the non-stretching “y” |
| One or both sides | Upper molars | ض | Heavy sound from the side of the tongue |
| Front edges | Gums behind the upper front teeth | ل | Light “l” |
| Tip | Gums of the upper front teeth, just below the ل | ن | “n” with a hum |
| Tip, slightly nearer the top | Gums of the upper front teeth | ر | A single tap, not the English “r” |
| Tip | Base of the upper front teeth | ط د ت | ط is heavy, ت is light |
| Tip | Close to the front teeth, with a small gap | ص ز س | The “whistling” letters; ص is heavy |
| Tip | Edges of the upper front teeth | ظ ذ ث | Tongue tip peeks out slightly; ظ is heavy |

### The tricky tongue pairs

- **ق vs ك.** ق is deeper and fuller. A fun pair: “قَلْب” (heart) and “كَلْب” (dog). Children never forget why this one matters.
- **ص vs س.** Same place, but ص is heavy with the back of the tongue raised. “صَيْف” (summer) and “سَيْف” (sword) is a good practice pair.
- **ط vs ت** and **ض vs د.** ط and ض are heavy; ت and د are light.
- **ث ذ ظ vs س ز.** English speakers often replace ث with “s” and ذ with “z.” The tongue tip must touch the edges of the top teeth for ث ذ ظ.
- **ر.** The Arabic ر is a light tap of the tongue tip. Children raised with English or French “r” often need extra practice.

The letter **ض** is often called the hardest Arabic letter. It comes from the side of the tongue pressing against the upper molars. Do not expect a young child to master it quickly. Gentle, regular correction from a teacher works best.

## 4. The lips: ash-shafatan

The lips have **two** makharij for four letters:

- **ف**: the inner part of the lower lip touches the tips of the upper front teeth.
- **ب م و**: both lips. For **ب** the lips close firmly; for **م** they close with a hum through the nose; for **و** (non-stretching “w”) they round without closing.

**Kid’s cue:** use a mirror. Ask your child to show you “ba,” “ma” and “wa” and watch the lips close, close-and-hum, and round.

## 5. The nasal passage: al-khayshum

The **khayshum** is the passage at the back of the nose. It is the source of **ghunnah**, the nasal hum that goes with ن and م, especially when they have a shaddah, as in “إِنَّ” and “ثُمَّ.”

**Kid’s cue:** pinch the nose gently while saying the hum. If the sound stops, the ghunnah is coming from the right place. Our guide to [tajweed rules for kids](/blog/tajweed-rules-for-kids) explains when ghunnah is held for two counts.

## Makharij for kids by age

| Age | Focus | What works |
|---|---|---|
| 3–5 | Hearing and copying sounds | Teacher models, child repeats; songs; mirrors |
| 5–7 | Letter-by-letter accuracy while learning to read | Qaida lessons, “a-letter” test, sound sorting games |
| 7–10 | Naming the five areas, fixing tricky pairs | Printed chart, minimal pairs, recording and listening back |
| 10+ | Full 17 makharij and letter qualities (sifat) | Structured tajweed study with a qualified teacher |

## Fun makharij games for home

1. **Mirror detective.** Sit side by side at a mirror. Say a letter and let your child guess which area it comes from by watching your mouth.
2. **Throat ladder.** Put a hand gently on the throat and climb: ء/ه at the bottom, ع/ح in the middle, غ/خ at the top.
3. **Sorting cards.** Write each letter on a card and sort them into five boxes labelled throat, tongue, lips, nose and empty space.
4. **Heavy or light?** Call out pairs (ص/س, ط/ت, ض/د, ظ/ذ). Your child stands tall for a heavy letter and crouches for a light one.
5. **Word swap.** Use pairs like قَلْب/كَلْب and صَيْف/سَيْف. Say one, and your child points to the matching picture.
6. **Record and replay.** Older children record themselves reading a short surah and listen for one letter, such as every ح.

Keep games to 5–10 minutes. Short, cheerful practice every day beats long drills.

## When to get extra help

Some children have difficulty with particular sounds in any language, for example a lisp or trouble with “r” in English too. If a sound problem shows up in English or French as well as Arabic, it may be worth speaking to a speech-language pathologist. In Ottawa, [First Words](https://www.firstwords.ca/) offers free preschool speech and language services for children from birth until school age, and [Speech-Language & Audiology Canada](https://www.sac-oac.ca/) lists practitioners across the country. A Quran teacher can then help apply the progress to Arabic letters.

For Arabic-specific sounds, a qualified Quran teacher who listens regularly is the most effective help. Our overviews of [Quran classes in Ottawa](/blog/quran-classes-for-kids-ottawa) and [Arabic classes for kids in Ottawa](/blog/arabic-classes-for-kids-ottawa) list options by area, and the guide to [tajweed classes in Ottawa](/blog/tajweed-classes-ottawa) explains what to ask a teacher.

## Final thoughts

Makharij can sound technical, but for children it is really a game of discovery: where does this sound live? Start with the five areas, use the “a-letter” test, practise the tricky pairs with a mirror, and let your child hear a good reciter every day. Clear letters are the foundation for everything that follows in Quran recitation.
