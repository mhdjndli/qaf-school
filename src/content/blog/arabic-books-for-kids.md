The best Arabic books for kids match their stage: sturdy first-word board books for babies and toddlers, short picture books for preschoolers, levelled readers for children learning to read, and richer stories for confident readers aged 8 and up. Look for Modern Standard Arabic text with full harakat (vowel marks) and, if you are still learning Arabic yourself, books with audio. Below is a stage-by-stage guide with verified series, publishers and award winners, plus where to find Arabic children's books in Canada.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Choose by reading stage, not just age. A 7-year-old who is new to Arabic still needs books with one or two words per page.
> - Full harakat matter for beginners. They show exactly how each word sounds, for children and for parents.
> - Levelled readers, such as the Ascend with Asala sets or digital libraries like I Read Arabic and 3asafeer, are the fastest route from letters to real reading.
> - Original Arabic books, not only translations, give children Arab stories, names and humour.
> - Reading aloud for 10 minutes a day does more than a big bookshelf that rarely gets opened.

## How to choose Arabic books for your child

### Check the harakat

Harakat are the small vowel marks above and below Arabic letters. Books for beginners should be fully vowelled, so a child can sound out every word. As children grow more confident, books gradually drop the marks, which is normal for adult Arabic. If you are teaching the vowels now, our guide to [teaching the Arabic alphabet to kids](/blog/teach-arabic-alphabet-to-kids) covers them step by step.

### Decide between fusha and dialect

Almost all published children's books use Modern Standard Arabic (fusha), the Arabic of school, books and news, closely related to the Arabic of the Quran. A few picture books use a spoken dialect. For reading and school success, fusha is the right base; our guide to [fusha vs dialect for kids](/blog/fusha-vs-dialect-for-kids) explains why.

### Count the words on a page

For a child just starting to read, one to five words per page is plenty. If your child has to stop on every word, the book is too hard to read alone, but it may still be perfect to read together.

### Look for audio if you are learning too

Many digital libraries include narration by native speakers, and some print books come with audio. This lets non-Arabic-speaking parents follow along and model correct pronunciation.

### Mix originals and translations

Translations of English favourites can be a fun bridge because your child already knows the story. Original Arabic books add something translations can't: Arab names, settings, food and humour that help children feel the language belongs to them.

## Board books and first words (ages 0–3)

At this stage, books are for pointing, naming and cuddling. One clear picture and one word per page is ideal.

### Adam & Mishmish board books (Al Salwa)

The characters from the popular Arabic song channel Adam Wa Mishmish also appear in books from [Al Salwa Publishers](https://alsalwabooks.com/), a Jordanian publisher of original Arabic children's books for ages 0 to 11 and up. Titles include *Colours*, *Animals*, *Animal Sounds*, *Head to Toe* and *It's Time for Bed*, plus alphabet flash cards.

**Best for:** Toddlers who already know the songs. Hearing the same words in a song and a book helps them stick. See our guide to [Arabic songs for kids](/blog/arabic-songs-for-kids) for the channel.

### Arabic picture dictionaries

A picture dictionary groups everyday words by theme: home, food, animals, school. The *Goodword Arabic Picture Dictionary for Kids (English–Arabic)* is one widely sold example, and it works well for bilingual families who want English and Arabic side by side.

**Best for:** Ages 2–6, and parents who want a quick reference for the words they use every day. Pair it with our list of [100 Arabic words for kids](/blog/arabic-vocabulary-for-kids), organized by theme.

## Picture books for preschool and kindergarten (ages 3–7)

Picture books are where children fall in love with stories in Arabic. Read them aloud, and let your child turn the pages and point.

### Original picture books from Al Salwa

Al Salwa's picture books for ages 3–5 include *Mora is Scared*, *The Biggest Rocket*, *Shanoona* and *I Am an Arab Child* (an Adam & Mishmish title). For ages 5–7, the list includes *Adam the Dream Catcher* and *Festival of Animals*, along with silent (wordless) books and craft books. The publisher's site lists books by age, which makes choosing easier.

### Award-winning Arabic picture books

The [Etisalat Award for Arabic Children's Literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etisalat_Award_for_Arabic_Children%27s_Literature), founded in 2009 and announced each year at the Sharjah International Book Fair, is a useful place to find well-made Arabic picture books. Past winners include:

- ***The Black Dot* (النقطة السوداء)** by Walid Taher, published by Dar El Shorouk in Egypt (2010 winner).
- ***Fly Away Kite*** by Amani Al Ashmawi, published by Nahdet Misr (2011 winner).
- ***Creatures on the Ceiling*** by Nabiha Mheidly, illustrated by Hassan Zahreddine, published by Hadaek Group in Lebanon (2012 winner).

Award winners are not always easy to find in Canada, so check online Arabic bookstores or ask a library to request them.

> **Tip:** In our classroom, we've found that children ask for the same Arabic picture book again and again. That is exactly what you want: each rereading adds words they recognize.

## Levelled readers for children learning to read (ages 4–9)

Levelled readers are short books written with carefully controlled vocabulary. Each level adds a few new words and slightly longer sentences, so children can read independently and feel successful. They are the single most useful type of Arabic book for a child learning to read.

### Ascend with Asala (اصعد مع أصالة)

**What it is:** A print levelled reading series from Dar Asala, sold in sets: an alphabet set followed by Levels 1, 2 and 3. According to [Maktabatee's listing](https://www.maktabatee.com/collections/leveled-readers), the Level 1 books run 8–16 pages each and move from one or two words per page to several words, introducing everyday vocabulary and grammar gradually. The listing also says the series received the Khalifa Award for Education.

**Best for:** Families and teachers who want a structured, paper-based path from letters to sentences.

**Our take:** A strong choice if you want your child off screens while learning to read. Sets sell out at times, so check current availability and price.

### I Read Arabic (أقرأ بالعربية)

**What it is:** A digital reading program from Little Thinking Minds, a Jordanian education technology company. [I Read Arabic](https://ar.ireadarabic.com/) covers kindergarten to Grade 12 with around 800 books, plus videos, games and comprehension activities. Families can start a free trial.

**Best for:** Children aged 4 and up who need lots of levelled books without filling a shelf.

**Our take:** The breadth is hard to match in print. The program is built mainly for children who speak Arabic at home, so if Arabic is new to your family, start at the lowest levels and read together.

### 3asafeer (عصافير)

**What it is:** A digital Arabic reading platform for ages 3 to 18 with stories graded from beginner to proficient. [3asafeer](https://3asafeer.com/) says its stories are narrated by professional actors in Modern Standard Arabic, and it offers a 7-day free trial for families.

**Best for:** Families with several children at different levels, and parents who want narration to follow along with.

**Our take:** The narration is especially helpful for parents who are still learning Arabic. For a full comparison of digital options, see our review of the [best Arabic apps for kids](/blog/best-arabic-apps-for-kids).

## Early chapter books and older readers (ages 7 and up)

Once children read short books confidently, they are ready for longer stories with chapters.

### Al Salwa's Dahnoun series (ages 7–9)

Al Salwa lists its Dahnoun series, including *My Grandfather's Gift* and *My Name is Hala*, as early readers for ages 7–9. Books at this level are a good step between picture books and full chapter books.

### Kamel Kilani's classic retellings (ages 9+)

Kamel Kilani (كامل كيلاني, 1897–1959) was an Egyptian writer widely regarded as the pioneer of Arabic children's literature. He began writing for children in 1927, starting with *Sinbad the Sailor* (السندباد البحري), and his retellings include *Aladdin's Lamp*, *Robinson Crusoe* and *Hayy ibn Yaqdhan*. Many editions are still in print.

**Our take:** The language is richer and more classical than modern picture books, so these work best for confident readers or read-alouds with a parent.

### Etisalat Award winners for young adults (ages 12+)

The Etisalat Award added a Best Young Adult Book category in 2013. Winners such as *Ajwan* by Noura Al Noman (Nahdet Misr) are a good place to look for teens who read Arabic well.

> **Islamic books in Arabic.** Many Arabic children's bookstores also carry Islamic picture books in Arabic about Allah's names, the Prophet ﷺ, duas and manners, a natural way to combine reading with faith. If your child reads more comfortably in English, see our list of the [best Islamic books for kids](/blog/best-islamic-books-for-kids).

## How to read Arabic books with your child

### Step 1: Preview the book yourself

Read it once on your own, or listen to the audio, so you know the words and the story before you read with your child.

### Step 2: Talk about the pictures first

Name what you see in Arabic, even if you only know a few words. This builds vocabulary before you read a single line.

### Step 3: Read aloud slowly, pointing to the words

Run your finger under each word from right to left. Children learn the direction of Arabic text and connect sounds to letters.

### Step 4: Let your child "read" the parts they know

Pause before a word your child knows and let them say it. With levelled readers, let them sound out the easy words on their own.

### Step 5: Reread, often

Children learn the most from the second, fifth and tenth reading of a book. Keep a small basket of Arabic books within reach and rotate them every few weeks.

## Where to find Arabic books for kids in Canada

- **Online Arabic children's bookstores.** Specialist stores such as Maktabatee sort books by age, have a levelled-reader section and stock publishers such as Al Salwa, Asala and Hadaek. Jarir Books USA lists worldwide shipping, including to Canada. Shipping costs and times vary, so compare before ordering.
- **Publishers directly.** Some publishers, such as Al Salwa, list their full catalogue online by age group, which is useful for planning even if you buy elsewhere.
- **Your public library.** Search the [Ottawa Public Library](https://biblioottawalibrary.ca/en) catalogue (or your own library's) for Arabic-language children's titles, and ask a librarian about requesting materials.
- **Digital libraries.** I Read Arabic and 3asafeer give access to hundreds of levelled books for one subscription, with no shipping.
- **Friends and family.** Grandparents visiting from overseas are often the best source of new Arabic books. Send them a list.
