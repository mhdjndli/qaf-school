The days of the week in Arabic are al-aḥad (Sunday), al-ithnayn (Monday), ath-thulāthāʾ (Tuesday), al-arbiʿāʾ (Wednesday), al-khamīs (Thursday), al-jumuʿah (Friday) and as-sabt (Saturday). Five of the seven names come straight from the Arabic numbers one to five, which makes them one of the easiest word sets a child can learn. Below you'll find a full table with harakat and pronunciation, the meaning behind each name, the phrases you need to actually use them, and games that make them stick.

> **Key takeaways**
> - The Arabic week is counted from Sunday: al-aḥad means "one," al-ithnayn "two," and so on up to al-khamīs, "five."
> - Friday (al-jumuʿah) means "the gathering," named for the congregational prayer; Saturday (as-sabt) is linked to the idea of rest.
> - Teach the days with yawm (day) in front: يَوْمُ الأَحَدِ, "the day of one," is how they are often said in full.
> - Children learn the days fastest when they say them every morning, attached to real events: Jumuʿah, Sunday class, a weekly treat.
> - For 4- and 5-year-olds, aim for reciting the sequence first; "yesterday" and "tomorrow" come with time.

## Days of the week in Arabic: the full table

Here are all seven days in the traditional Arabic order, which starts on Sunday. Each name is shown on its own and with يَوْم (yawm, "day") in front, the way your child will often hear it.

| English | Arabic | With "day" | Pronunciation | Literal link |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sunday | الأَحَد | يَوْمُ الأَحَدِ | al-aḥad | one (أَحَد) |
| Monday | الاِثْنَيْن | يَوْمُ الاِثْنَيْنِ | al-ithnayn | two (اِثْنَان) |
| Tuesday | الثُّلَاثَاء | يَوْمُ الثُّلَاثَاءِ | ath-thulāthāʾ | three (ثَلَاثَة) |
| Wednesday | الأَرْبِعَاء | يَوْمُ الأَرْبِعَاءِ | al-arbiʿāʾ | four (أَرْبَعَة) |
| Thursday | الخَمِيس | يَوْمُ الخَمِيسِ | al-khamīs | five (خَمْسَة) |
| Friday | الجُمُعَة | يَوْمُ الجُمُعَةِ | al-jumuʿah | gathering (جَمَعَ) |
| Saturday | السَّبْت | يَوْمُ السَّبْتِ | as-sabt | rest |

The word for "week" is أُسْبُوع (usbūʿ), and "the days of the week" is أَيَّامُ الأُسْبُوعِ (ayyām al-usbūʿ). You'll notice usbūʿ shares its root with سَبْعَة (sabʿah), "seven." A week is literally "a seven."

**A spelling note for parents:** you will often see Monday written الإثنين, with a hamza under the alif. The more careful spelling is الاثنين, because the alif is a connecting alif (hamzat al-waṣl). Either way, it is pronounced al-ithnayn. Don't let this worry a young child; it matters more once they start writing.

## The meaning behind each Arabic day name

Children remember words better when the word has a story. The Arabic day names have a very simple one.

### Sunday to Thursday: counting from one to five

The first five days are counted. If your child already knows a few [Arabic numbers for kids](/blog/arabic-numbers-for-kids), they already know most of the week:

- **الأَحَد (al-aḥad), Sunday:** from أَحَد, "one." It is the same word in قُلْ هُوَ اللَّهُ أَحَدٌ, "Say, He is Allah, the One" (Surah Al-Ikhlas 112:1). Many children light up when they spot it.
- **الاِثْنَيْن (al-ithnayn), Monday:** from اِثْنَان, "two."
- **الثُّلَاثَاء (ath-thulāthāʾ), Tuesday:** from ثَلَاثَة, "three."
- **الأَرْبِعَاء (al-arbiʿāʾ), Wednesday:** from أَرْبَعَة, "four."
- **الخَمِيس (al-khamīs), Thursday:** from خَمْسَة, "five."

A simple way to show this: hold up one finger and say "al-aḥad," two fingers and say "al-ithnayn," up to five. By the time the hand is open, your child has said Sunday to Thursday.

### Friday: the day of gathering

**الجُمُعَة (al-jumuʿah)** comes from the root ج-م-ع, "to gather." Friday is the day Muslims gather for the congregational Jumuʿah prayer. The name appears in the Quran itself:

يَا أَيُّهَا الَّذِينَ آمَنُوا إِذَا نُودِيَ لِلصَّلَاةِ مِن يَوْمِ الْجُمُعَةِ فَاسْعَوْا إِلَىٰ ذِكْرِ اللَّهِ

"O you who believe, when the call is made for the prayer on the day of Jumuʿah, hasten to the remembrance of Allah" ([Surah Al-Jumuʿah 62:9](https://quran.com/62/9)). A whole surah of the Quran carries this day's name.

The Prophet ﷺ also told us that Friday is the best day on which the sun rises, and that Adam was created on it ([Sahih Muslim 854](https://sunnah.com/muslim:854a)). For a child, "Friday is the best day of the week" is a lovely, simple fact to hold on to.

### Saturday: the day of rest

**السَّبْت (as-sabt)** comes from the root س-ب-ت, which is linked with rest. The same root appears in the Quran: وَجَعَلْنَا نَوْمَكُمْ سُبَاتًا, "And made your sleep [a means for] rest" ([Surah An-Naba 78:9](https://quran.com/78/9)). For a young child, "as-sabt is a resting day" is enough.

## Useful phrases: today, tomorrow and yesterday in Arabic

Knowing seven words is a start. Using them in a sentence is what makes them part of your child's Arabic. These are the phrases we'd teach alongside the days.

| English | Arabic | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| day / days | يَوْم / أَيَّام | yawm / ayyām |
| week | أُسْبُوع | usbūʿ |
| today | اليَوْمَ | al-yawm |
| yesterday | أَمْسِ | ams |
| tomorrow | غَدًا | ghadan |
| the day after tomorrow | بَعْدَ غَدٍ | baʿda ghad |
| weekend | نِهَايَةُ الأُسْبُوعِ | nihāyat al-usbūʿ |
| What day is it today? | مَا اليَوْمُ؟ | mā al-yawm? |
| Today is Sunday. | اليَوْمُ يَوْمُ الأَحَدِ. | al-yawmu yawmu l-aḥad |
| Tomorrow is Monday. | غَدًا يَوْمُ الاِثْنَيْنِ. | ghadan yawmu l-ithnayn |
| Yesterday was Saturday. | أَمْسِ كَانَ يَوْمَ السَّبْتِ. | amsi kāna yawma s-sabt |
| on Friday | يَوْمَ الجُمُعَةِ | yawma l-jumuʿah |

The pattern "اليَوْمُ يَوْمُ ..." (today is the day of...) is the one to drill. Once your child can answer مَا اليَوْمُ؟ every morning, they are using Arabic, not just reciting it.

## Days of the week in the Sunnah

The days of the week aren't just vocabulary for a Muslim child. Several of them carry meaning in our deen, and that gives you natural reasons to talk about them.

- **Monday:** The Prophet ﷺ was asked about fasting on Mondays and said it was the day he was born and the day revelation came to him ([Sahih Muslim 1162](https://sunnah.com/muslim:1162b)).
- **Monday and Thursday:** He ﷺ said that deeds are presented to Allah on Mondays and Thursdays, and that he loved for his deeds to be presented while he was fasting ([Jami' at-Tirmidhi 747](https://sunnah.com/tirmidhi:747), graded hasan).
- **Friday:** Jumuʿah prayer, the best day of the week (Sahih Muslim 854) and the day named in Surah Al-Jumuʿah.

You don't need to ask a five-year-old to fast. But telling them "Dad fasts on al-ithnayn and al-khamīs, just like the Prophet ﷺ did" connects two Arabic words to something real in your home. That is exactly how vocabulary becomes meaningful.

## You might hear different versions: fusha and dialects

The table above is Modern Standard Arabic (fusha), the Arabic of books, school and the Quran. In everyday speech, the names are often shortened or pronounced a little differently from country to country. For example, in Cairo the letter ج is pronounced as a hard "g," so Friday sounds closer to "el-gumʿa."

If grandparents use a dialect, that's a gift, not a problem. Children handle both well when adults are consistent. Our post on [fusha vs dialect for kids](/blog/fusha-vs-dialect-for-kids) explains how to decide which to prioritise.

It's also worth knowing that "weekend" means different days in different countries. Canada's school week runs Monday to Friday, while in Saudi Arabia, for example, the weekend is Friday and Saturday. If your child talks to cousins abroad, this can lead to a fun conversation about why Friday is so special.

## How to teach the days of the week in Arabic: step by step

Most children start reciting the days in order around kindergarten age, while real understanding of "yesterday" and "next week" develops gradually over the next few years. So start with the sequence and grow from there.

### Step 1: Teach the numbers one to five first

If your child can count وَاحِد، اِثْنَان، ثَلَاثَة، أَرْبَعَة، خَمْسَة, half the job is done. Spend a few days on counting before the days. Then show the link: "al-khamīs sounds like khamsah!"

### Step 2: Learn the sequence as a chant

Say the seven days in order with a clap on each one: الأَحَد (clap), الاِثْنَيْن (clap), الثُّلَاثَاء (clap), and so on. Then try it to a tune your child already knows. Any simple melody works; the rhythm is what helps memory. Many families find a two-line chant easiest: Sunday to Wednesday on the first line, Thursday to Saturday on the second.

### Step 3: Ask مَا اليَوْمُ؟ every morning

Make it part of breakfast or the drive to school. Ask "مَا اليَوْمُ؟" and help your child answer "اليَوْمُ يَوْمُ ..." Point to a calendar if you have one. After two or three weeks of this, most children start answering without help.

### Step 4: Anchor each day to something real

Abstract words stick when they're tied to events. Build your own family week in Arabic:

- الجُمُعَة: Jumuʿah prayer, a special breakfast, or a visit to the masjid.
- الأَحَد: Sunday class or a family outing.
- الخَمِيس: library day, or the night you prepare clothes for Jumuʿah.

### Step 5: Add yesterday and tomorrow

Once the sequence is solid, add أَمْسِ and غَدًا: "اليَوْمُ يَوْمُ الخَمِيسِ. غَدًا يَوْمُ ...?" Let your child finish the sentence. This is harder than it looks, because it asks them to think in sequence, so be patient.

### Step 6: Read and write the names (ages 5+)

When your child can recognise letters, label a weekly chart with the Arabic names. Start by matching printed cards to the chart, then copying one day a week. Our guide to [Arabic handwriting for kids](/blog/arabic-writing-for-kids) covers letter formation if they're ready.

> **Teacher tip:** Don't drill all seven days in a single sitting with a young child. Two minutes every morning beats twenty minutes on a Saturday. Repetition across days is the whole point.

## 8 games to practise the Arabic days of the week

Short, playful practice works best. All of these take five minutes or less. For more ideas, see our list of [Arabic learning games](/blog/arabic-games-for-kids).

1. **Fingers to five:** Count Sunday to Thursday on one hand, then add a hand-clap for al-jumuʿah and a "sleeping" head-tilt for as-sabt.
2. **What's missing?** Lay seven day cards in order, have your child close their eyes, remove one, and ask which day is missing.
3. **Line up:** Shuffle the cards and race to put them back in order. Time it and try to beat yesterday's score.
4. **Day hopscotch:** Chalk seven squares outside and say each day as you hop.
5. **Before and after:** "What comes after الثُّلَاثَاء?" "What comes before الجُمُعَة?" Older kids can quiz you back.
6. **Weekly picture planner:** Draw one thing you do each day and label it in Arabic. Hang it on the fridge.
7. **Day detective:** Look at the calendar and ask "What day is Eid?" or "What day is Grandma's birthday?" Our [Islamic months for kids](/blog/islamic-months-for-kids) guide pairs well with this.
8. **Story chain:** Take turns adding a sentence: "يَوْمَ الأَحَدِ we went to the park. يَوْمَ الاِثْنَيْنِ..." Silly stories are encouraged.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Starting with Monday.** English calendars often start on Monday. In Arabic, start on Sunday so the counting logic works.
- **Skipping the "al-".** Teach the full name with the article: الأَحَد, not just أَحَد, which simply means "one."
- **Mixing up Tuesday and Wednesday.** ath-thulāthāʾ and al-arbiʿāʾ both end in "-āʾ." Link them to three and four fingers.
- **Correcting too much.** If your child says "al-ithnayn" when it's Tuesday, say "اليَوْمُ يَوْمُ الثُّلَاثَاءِ!" cheerfully and move on.
- **Teaching only from a screen.** Videos are fine for listening, but speaking the days to a real person, every day, is what builds the habit.
