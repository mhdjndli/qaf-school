A hifz tracker is a simple record of what your child has memorized, when each portion was last revised, and how strong it is right now. It can be a colouring chart on the fridge, a notebook, a spreadsheet, an app or a teacher’s parent portal. The format matters less than the habit: a hifz tracker that is updated every day shows you exactly what to revise before anything is forgotten.

This guide explains what to record, compares the main types of hifz trackers, and gives you ready-to-copy templates for young children, school-age kids and teens.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Track three things for every portion: date memorized, date last revised and current strength.
> - Young children need a visual chart; older children need a daily log; teens can manage a strength grid or app.
> - Rate strength with a simple three-level scale (strong, okay, weak) and revise the weak portions first.
> - Apps and portals help, but a teacher should still listen regularly.
> - Keep tracking encouraging: it is a map for revision, not a report card.

## Why track hifz progress at all?

**Tracking matters because memorized Quran fades without regular revision, and it is impossible to remember which surah was last revised three weeks ago.** The Prophet ﷺ said: “Commit yourself to the Qur’an, for by Him in whose Hand is my soul, it is surely more prone to break away than a camel in its bind” ([Sahih al-Bukhari 5033](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:5033)).

A tracker turns that hadith into a practical system. It shows you:

- Which portions are due for revision today.
- Which surahs keep slipping and need extra attention.
- Whether your child is adding new lessons faster than they can keep them.
- How far they have come, which is a huge motivator on hard days.

The Prophet ﷺ also taught that the most beloved deed to Allah is the most regular and constant, even if it is small ([Sahih al-Bukhari 6464](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:6464)). A tracker makes consistency visible.

## What a hifz tracker should record

Whatever format you use, these are the columns that make a hifz tracker useful.

| Column | What to write | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Portion** | Surah name and ayat (or page/juz for older students) | Defines exactly what was memorized |
| **Date memorized** | When the portion was first completed | Shows pace over time |
| **Last revised** | The most recent date it was recited from memory | The single most important column |
| **Strength** | Strong / okay / weak | Tells you what to revise first |
| **Mistakes** | Specific ayat or words that trip your child up | Targets practice precisely |
| **Teacher notes** | Tajweed points or corrections | Keeps home practice aligned with class |

For a daily log, add **sabaq** (new lesson), **sabqi** (recent revision) and **manzil** (older revision). Our [hifz revision schedule](/blog/hifz-revision-schedule) explains how to balance these three.

## Types of hifz trackers compared

| Tracker type | Best for | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Colouring or sticker chart** | Ages 4–7 | Visual, fun, child-owned | Little detail on strength or mistakes |
| **Notebook log** | Ages 7–12 | Cheap, flexible, teacher can write in it | Easy to lose; harder to see patterns |
| **Spreadsheet** | Parents of older kids, teens | Sorts by “last revised”, easy to share | Needs a device; less fun for children |
| **App** | Teens who recite independently | Some apps detect mistakes and track streaks | Premium features may cost money; screen time |
| **Program portal** | Families with a halaqah or school | Teacher records progress; parents see it at home | Only shows class lessons, not home revision |

Most families end up combining two: a program portal or teacher’s notebook for class progress, plus a chart or log at home for daily revision.

## How to set up a hifz tracker: step by step

**Step 1: List everything already memorized.** Sit with your child and write every surah they know, starting with Al-Fatiha. Do not trust memory; have them recite each one.

**Step 2: Rate each portion.** Use the strength scale below. Be honest; this is a starting map, not a judgment.

**Step 3: Choose the format.** Pick the format that fits your child’s age from the table above. Keep it somewhere visible, near where you do Quran time.

**Step 4: Build a revision rotation.** Divide memorized portions into groups so everything is revised at least once a week. Weak portions go into the rotation more often.

**Step 5: Update it daily, in under a minute.** After each session, write the date beside what was revised and adjust the strength if needed.

**Step 6: Review it weekly with the teacher.** Share the tracker with your child’s teacher or check it against the class portal. Ask which portions the teacher thinks need work.

### A simple strength scale

Clear criteria make ratings consistent, whether a parent, sibling or teacher is listening.

| Rating | What it sounds like | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Strong** | Recites smoothly from a random starting point, with no more than one small slip | Revise on the normal rotation |
| **Okay** | Recites from the beginning but hesitates or needs one or two prompts | Revise twice this week |
| **Weak** | Needs several prompts, mixes up ayat or skips lines | Revise daily, and pause new lessons if many are weak |

**A useful rule: if more than a quarter of what your child knows is rated weak, stop adding new lessons until revision catches up.**

## Hifz tracker templates you can copy

### Template 1: Juz Amma colouring chart (ages 4–7)

Draw a ladder or a tree with one space for each surah, starting with Al-Fatiha and then An-Nas. Your child colours a space when they memorize a surah and adds a star each time it is revised. The first steps look like this:

| Surah | Ayat | Memorized | Revised this week |
|---|---|---|---|
| Al-Fatihah (1) | 7 | ☐ | ☐ ☐ ☐ |
| An-Nas (114) | 6 | ☐ | ☐ ☐ ☐ |
| Al-Falaq (113) | 5 | ☐ | ☐ ☐ ☐ |
| Al-Ikhlas (112) | 4 | ☐ | ☐ ☐ ☐ |
| Al-Masad (111) | 5 | ☐ | ☐ ☐ ☐ |
| An-Nasr (110) | 3 | ☐ | ☐ ☐ ☐ |
| Al-Kafirun (109) | 6 | ☐ | ☐ ☐ ☐ |
| Al-Kawthar (108) | 3 | ☐ | ☐ ☐ ☐ |
| Al-Ma‘un (107) | 7 | ☐ | ☐ ☐ ☐ |
| Quraysh (106) | 4 | ☐ | ☐ ☐ ☐ |
| Al-Fil (105) | 5 | ☐ | ☐ ☐ ☐ |

Continue the same way through the rest of Juz Amma, back to An-Naba (78). For a full suggested order and pace, see our [Juz Amma memorization plan](/blog/juz-amma-memorization-plan).

### Template 2: Daily hifz log (ages 7–12)

| Date | Sabaq (new) | Sabqi (recent) | Manzil (older) | Strength | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mon | Al-‘Adiyat 1–3 | Al-Qari‘ah | Al-Fil, Quraysh | Okay | Mixed up ayah 2 |
| Tue | Al-‘Adiyat 4–6 | Al-Qari‘ah, Al-‘Adiyat 1–3 | Al-Ma‘un, Al-Kawthar | Strong | |
| Wed | Revision day | Al-‘Adiyat 1–6 | Al-Kafirun, An-Nasr | Strong | Teacher: watch the heavy letters |

One line per day is enough. The teacher can initial it at class.

### Template 3: Strength grid (teens and advanced students)

For students memorizing beyond Juz Amma, track by page or quarter-juz instead of surah. Make a grid with one cell per page and shade it green (strong), yellow (okay) or red (weak). A quick look shows where the red clusters are.

For a student who has memorized a lot, a monthly cycle is a helpful target. The Prophet ﷺ advised Abdullah ibn ‘Amr to recite the whole Quran in a month ([Sahih al-Bukhari 5054](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:5054)); a student can plan their manzil so that everything they have memorized is revised within a set cycle that their teacher agrees.

## Keeping tracking motivating, not stressful

- **Let your child own it.** Children who colour or tick their own chart care more about it.
- **Celebrate the revision column, not just new surahs.** A full week of revision deserves praise too.
- **Never use the tracker to shame.** A red row is a revision plan, not a failure.
- **Keep it private.** Avoid posting comparisons between siblings or classmates.
- **Pair data with dua.** End each week by asking Allah to make the Quran firm in your child’s heart.
