Muslim kids and Christmas at school can go together calmly. Most public-school classrooms in Canada mark the season with winter concerts, crafts, gift exchanges and maybe a visit from Santa. In Ontario you have the right to ask for religious accommodation. Most families find a middle path. They are kind and respectful toward classmates who celebrate, and clear with their child about what Muslims believe and why the family doesn't celebrate Christmas.

This guide covers how to talk to your child, what Ontario policy says, and how to work with the teacher. You will also find a sample note and ideas for keeping December warm at home.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Respect is part of Islam. The Quran tells Muslims to treat peaceful neighbours of other faiths with kindness and justice (Surah Al-Mumtahanah 60:8), and Muslims honour Prophet Isa (Jesus), peace be upon him, as one of the greatest prophets.
> - Most Muslim scholars advise against joining the religious worship of another faith. Views differ on cultural details such as greetings and winter parties, so ask a trusted local imam about the grey areas.
> - Ontario school boards must accommodate creed-based needs up to the point of undue hardship. Ask early, in writing, and suggest a simple alternative.
> - Children cope best when they know the plan in advance and have something joyful of their own to look forward to.

## Muslim kids and Christmas at school: what happens in class

In most Ontario public schools, December activities are cultural and seasonal rather than worship. Even so, many have Christian roots. The table below shows what families commonly run into and the questions worth asking.

| Activity | Usually religious? | Common family approaches |
|---|---|---|
| Winter or holiday concert | Varies by song list | Ask for the song list; request a different role or a non-religious piece if needed |
| Christmas crafts (trees, ornaments, Santa) | Mostly cultural | Ask for a winter alternative (snowflakes, mittens, snowmen) |
| Gift exchange / Secret Santa | Cultural | Join as a "winter gift exchange," or opt out politely |
| Classroom party with treats | Cultural | Send a halal, nut-free treat so your child can share |
| Nativity play or church visit | Religious | Most families request an alternative activity |
| Letters to Santa | Cultural/folk | Swap for a thank-you letter to a family member or a winter story |

Every family draws its own lines. What matters most is that you decide ahead of time, explain your choice to your child, and talk to the teacher before the activity happens, not after.

## What Islam teaches about Jesus and other faiths

Your child's first questions are often "Why don't *we* do Christmas?" and "Are my friends wrong?" Start with what Muslims share with Christian neighbours, then explain how the beliefs differ.

**Muslims love and respect Prophet Isa.** The Quran gives a whole surah to his mother, Maryam (Mary), and describes his miraculous birth. In Surah Maryam, the infant Isa speaks:

وَٱلسَّلَـٰمُ عَلَىَّ يَوْمَ وُلِدتُّ وَيَوْمَ أَمُوتُ وَيَوْمَ أُبْعَثُ حَيًّا

"Peace be upon me the day I was born, the day I die, and the day I am raised back to life" ([Surah Maryam 19:33](https://quran.com/19/33)). Prophet Muhammad ﷺ described himself as the closest of people to the son of Maryam and said that the prophets are brothers whose religion is one (Sahih al-Bukhari 3442–3443).

**Muslims believe Isa is a prophet, not God or the son of God.** That is the main reason Muslims don't celebrate Christmas as a religious festival. Children can hold both ideas at once: "We love Prophet Isa, and we believe he was a messenger of Allah. Our friends love him too, and they believe something different about him."

**Kindness to neighbours is a command, not a compromise.** Allah says:

لَّا يَنْهَىٰكُمُ ٱللَّهُ عَنِ ٱلَّذِينَ لَمْ يُقَـٰتِلُوكُمْ فِى ٱلدِّينِ وَلَمْ يُخْرِجُوكُم مِّن دِيَـٰرِكُمْ أَن تَبَرُّوهُمْ وَتُقْسِطُوٓا۟ إِلَيْهِمْ ۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ يُحِبُّ ٱلْمُقْسِطِينَ

"Allah does not forbid you from being kind and just to those who have not fought you over religion or driven you from your homes. Allah loves those who are just" ([Surah Al-Mumtahanah 60:8](https://quran.com/60/8)).

### Where scholars agree, and where they differ

Mainstream scholarship broadly agrees that Muslims should not take part in the religious rituals or worship of another faith. Scholars and communities differ on cultural details, though. These include seasonal greetings, eating at a classroom party and joining a secular gift exchange. This guide doesn't try to settle those questions. For your family's specific situation, ask a local imam or a scholar you trust, and avoid judging families who reach a different, sincere conclusion.

## How to talk to your child, by age

**Ages 3–5.** Keep it short and positive. "Some families have Christmas. Our family has Eid. Both families love their celebrations." Young children mostly want to know what *they* get to do, so focus on your own traditions. If they're starting at a public or Catholic school this year, read a picture book about Prophet Isa together or tell the story of Maryam.

**Ages 6–9.** Children this age notice fairness. Explain the "why" in one or two sentences, and role-play what to say when a friend asks, "What are you getting for Christmas?" A good answer is friendly and confident: "We don't celebrate Christmas. We celebrate Eid, and I get presents then! What are you doing for the holidays?"

**Ages 10–15.** Tweens and teens can handle the real discussion, including what scholars say and how to be a good friend without compromising beliefs. Ask what *they* think, and listen first. Our guide to [Muslim teens' identity in Canada](/blog/islamic-identity-teens-canada) goes deeper on that conversation.

At every age, two messages matter most:

1. **Your friends are not bad for celebrating.** Respect is part of your faith.
2. **You are not missing out.** Islam has its own beautiful days, and your family will make them special.

## Your rights in Ontario schools

Ontario public schools are expected to accommodate students' religious beliefs and practices. The [Ontario Human Rights Commission's policy statement on religious accommodation in schools](https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/policy-statement-religious-accommodation-schools) says education providers must accommodate creed-related needs to the point of undue hardship. Only three factors count when deciding hardship: cost, outside sources of funding, and health and safety. Inconvenience or other people's preferences are not valid reasons to refuse. The Commission's broader [Policy on preventing discrimination based on creed](https://www.ohrc.on.ca/en/policy-preventing-discrimination-based-creed) sets out the same principles.

School boards apply this through their own policies. The Ottawa-Carleton District School Board's religious accommodation policy (P.086.CUR) lists areas where families may ask for accommodation. These include holy days, prayer, dietary requirements and "participation in daily activities and Ontario curricula." The board also publishes a [calendar of religious and creed-based days of observance](https://www.ocdsb.ca/news-events/calendar/religious-creed-based-days-of-observance) and avoids scheduling major events on its most significant dates, including Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha.

A few practical points:

- **Accommodation is a conversation.** The school's goal is to include your child while respecting your beliefs, so expect to agree on an alternative rather than simply remove them.
- **It works both ways.** The OHRC notes that families can't expect respect for their own beliefs unless they also respect the beliefs of others. That is very much in line with Islamic adab.
- **Catholic boards are different.** Faith formation is central at a Catholic school, and Christmas is a major religious season there. If your child attends a Catholic school, ask the principal directly which exemptions or alternatives are available. The rules and practices differ from public boards, especially for elementary grades.

The same accommodation process covers other needs such as prayer space and Ramadan. Our guide to [Muslim kids in public school](/blog/muslim-kids-in-public-school) covers those in detail.

## Step-by-step: working with your child's teacher

Most teachers want every child to feel included, and many simply haven't been asked before. A friendly, early note solves most problems.

**Step 1: Decide your family's lines.** Before December, sit down together and agree on what your child will and won't do: the concert, crafts, gift exchange and party.

**Step 2: Ask what's planned.** In early November, email the teacher to ask about December activities, including song lists and any field trips.

**Step 3: Write a short, warm note.** Thank the teacher, explain your request in one or two sentences, and offer an alternative.

**Step 4: Offer to help.** Volunteer a winter craft, a story about Eid during a "celebrations around the world" unit, or a halal, nut-free treat for the party.

**Step 5: Prepare your child.** Tell them exactly what will happen and what they'll do instead, so it never feels like a punishment.

**Step 6: Follow up and say thank you.** A quick thank-you after the event builds goodwill for Ramadan and Eid later in the year.

> **Sample note to the teacher:** "Dear Ms. ___, thank you for all you do for our class. Our family is Muslim, and we don't celebrate Christmas as a religious holiday. Yusuf is welcome to join winter activities like snowflake crafts and the class party. For the nativity song and Christmas ornaments, could he do a winter alternative instead? We're happy to send a treat or share how we celebrate Eid this spring. Thank you for helping him feel included!"

## Gifts, cards and class parties

**Teacher gifts.** Giving gifts is a sunnah in its own right. The Prophet ﷺ said, "Give gifts and you will love one another" (Al-Adab al-Mufrad 594, graded hasan). Many Muslim families give teachers an end-of-term thank-you gift with a "Happy Holidays" or "Thank you for a wonderful term" card. That shows appreciation without religious symbolism. Others wait and give gifts at Eid or the end of the school year. Both are reasonable.

**Gift exchanges.** If your family is comfortable with a secular winter gift exchange, keep the gift neutral, like a book, art supplies or a puzzle. If not, ask the teacher whether your child can give small treats to everyone instead, so they still feel part of the fun.

**Class parties.** Send a halal, nut-free treat your child can share proudly. Check ingredient lists on candy canes and gummies, which can contain gelatin.

**Greetings.** When someone wishes your child "Merry Christmas," a smile and "Thank you, happy holidays!" is a kind, widely used reply. Whether Muslims may *initiate* Christmas greetings is one of the questions scholars differ on, so follow the guidance your family trusts.

## Making December special at home

Children feel left out when December is all about what they *can't* do. Give them something of their own.

- **Celebrate the break, not the holiday.** The [Ottawa Catholic School Board calendar](https://www.ocsb.ca/parentportal/) shows winter break running from December 21, 2026 to January 1, 2027, with students back on January 4. Most Ontario boards use a similar window, so check your own board's calendar. Plan family outings, a sleepover with cousins or a "winter Quran challenge." Our [winter break activities in Ottawa](/blog/winter-break-activities-ottawa) guide has a full list.
- **Tell the story of Maryam and Isa** from the Quran, over several nights, using Surah Maryam.
- **Count down to something Islamic.** Make a paper chain or calendar counting down to Ramadan and Eid al-Fitr in 2027. Ramadan is expected to begin around February 8, 2027, with Eid al-Fitr around March 9 or 10, depending on moon sighting.
- **Give in the season of giving.** Food bank donations and winter coat drives are a beautiful way to practise sadaqah. See our [sadaqah ideas for kids](/blog/charity-activities-for-kids).
- **Make Eid unforgettable.** When Eid comes, decorate, give gifts and invite friends, including non-Muslim classmates. Our [Eid gift ideas for kids](/blog/eid-gift-ideas-for-kids) can help you plan ahead.
