A good Arabic school for kids has three things you can check in one visit: a clear curriculum with levels, teachers who know how to teach children (not just speak Arabic), and enough weekly time on task for real progress. Everything else, from the building to the brochure, matters less. This guide gives you a practical way to evaluate any Arabic program, weekday or weekend, in Ottawa or anywhere else.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Decide your goal first: Quran reading, Arabic literacy, conversation, or all three. Different programs are built for different goals.
> - Ask to see the curriculum and level system. "We follow the teacher's book" is not a plan.
> - A native speaker is not automatically a good teacher of children. Ask about training and experience with your child's age.
> - Do the math on hours: one 45-minute class a week adds up to roughly 25 hours a year, which builds reading but not fluency.
> - Sit in on a class. Watch who is talking, how much Arabic is used, and how mistakes are corrected.

## What a good Arabic school for kids actually does

An Arabic school for kids is any structured program, weekday or weekend, in person or online, that teaches children to read, write, understand and speak Arabic in a planned sequence. The best ones do more than run lessons. They place each child at the right level, teach in a predictable order, check progress, and keep parents informed.

That sounds obvious, but many programs are run by dedicated volunteers with very little structure behind them. They can still be warm and worthwhile. You just want to know what you are signing up for, so your expectations match what the program can deliver.

In our classrooms we have found that children make the fastest progress when three conditions meet: lessons build on each other week to week, children hear and use a lot of Arabic in each session, and someone at home spends a few minutes a day on practice. A program can control the first two. The third is on you, and a good school will tell you exactly what to do.

## Types of Arabic programs at a glance

Before comparing schools, it helps to know which kind of program you are comparing. Each format suits different ages, goals and family schedules.

| Program type | Typical ages | Weekly time | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Arabic immersion kindergarten | 4–5 | Daily, all week | Natural listening, speaking, early reading | Limited to young ages; full-time commitment |
| Weekend Arabic or Islamic school | 5–15 | 2–4 hours, often shared with Quran and Islamic studies | Literacy plus community | Arabic time can shrink when the day covers many subjects |
| School board language program | JK–Grade 8 (varies by board) | About 2.5 hours on Saturdays, or after-school sessions | Low-cost, structured literacy | Large or mixed-level groups; check the board's current list |
| Online group class | 6+ | 1–3 sessions a week | Families without local options | Screen fatigue; less speaking time per child |
| Private tutor | Any | Flexible | Catching up, heritage learners, specific goals | Cost; quality varies widely |

None of these is "best" on its own. A five-year-old in an immersion kindergarten and a ten-year-old in a weekend school can both make excellent progress if the program fits their stage.

## Start with your goal, not the school

The most common mistake parents make is choosing a program before deciding what they want their child to be able to do. Ask yourself which of these matters most over the next two years:

1. **Reading the Quran fluently.** Your child needs strong letter recognition, harakat and smooth decoding. Many Quran programs teach this well, even if they teach little spoken Arabic.
2. **Arabic literacy.** Reading and writing Modern Standard Arabic (fusha) with growing vocabulary and understanding.
3. **Speaking and understanding.** Conversation with grandparents, cousins or classmates. This needs much more listening and speaking time than most weekly classes provide.
4. **Understanding the Quran's meaning.** Vocabulary and grammar in classical Arabic, usually from around age 10 and up.

Most families want a mix, and that is fine. But it changes the questions you ask. A program that promises conversational fluency in one hour a week is overpromising. A program built for Quran reading may be exactly right if that is your priority.

Two related decisions shape your search: whether your child should learn standard Arabic or a dialect (see [fusha vs dialect for kids](/blog/fusha-vs-dialect-for-kids)), and whether your child already understands Arabic at home but cannot read it, which calls for a different approach covered in our guide to [heritage Arabic speakers](/blog/arabic-for-heritage-speakers).

## 10 things to look for in an Arabic program

Use these as a scorecard when you call, visit or sit in on a class.

### 1. A written curriculum with clear levels

Ask: "What will my child be able to do by June?" A strong program can answer in concrete terms, such as "read short vowelled sentences" or "write all letters in their connected forms." It should also have levels or groups, with a sense of what moves a child from one to the next.

### 2. A placement check

Children arrive with very different skills. A short reading and listening check on the first day, or before enrolment, prevents a common problem: a confident reader stuck reviewing the alphabet, or a beginner lost in a class reading paragraphs.

### 3. Teachers trained to teach children

Fluency in Arabic is a starting point, not a qualification. Ask how teachers are trained, how long they have taught your child's age group, and how they handle children who are shy, restless or behind. A good teacher of six-year-olds uses movement, songs, games and short tasks. A good teacher of 12-year-olds sets real challenges and explains the "why."

### 4. How much Arabic is spoken in class

Children learn a language by hearing and using it. Watch whether the teacher gives instructions in simple Arabic, with gestures and pictures, or explains everything in English. Some English is normal with beginners, especially older ones. But a class where Arabic appears only on the worksheet is a reading class, not a language class.

### 5. A balance of reading, writing, listening and speaking

Language educators usually describe proficiency across four skills: listening, speaking, reading and writing. The [ACTFL Proficiency Guidelines](https://www.actfl.org/proficiency-guidelines-overview), widely used in North American language teaching, describe each skill separately because learners often develop them unevenly. Ask how the program covers all four, and which one it prioritizes at your child's age.

### 6. Class size and grouping

Smaller groups mean more turns to read aloud and speak. There is no magic number, but ask how many children are in the class, whether there is an assistant, and whether children are grouped by level, by age, or both. A wide age mix can work with a skilled teacher; a wide level mix is harder.

### 7. Enough time on task

Time adds up slowly. Do the quick math in the next section before you decide whether a program can meet your goals.

### 8. Regular progress updates

You should not have to wait until June to learn whether your child can read. Ask how progress is tracked (reading checks, portfolios, report cards, an app) and how often you will hear from the teacher.

### 9. Good materials

Look at the books. Are they designed for children learning Arabic, with clear print, full harakat and engaging pictures? Are there levelled readers? Photocopied sheets are not a deal-breaker, but a program with a real series of books usually has a real sequence too.

### 10. A safe, well-run environment

Ask who supervises children during breaks, how pick-up works, and whether teachers and volunteers have had a police check. In Ottawa, adults in positions of trust with children typically need a [Level 3 Vulnerable Sector Check from the Ottawa Police Service](https://www.ottawapolice.ca/en/reports-and-services/police-record-and-background-checks/police-record-checks/). A well-run school will answer these questions without hesitation.

## Do the math: hours per year

Language learning is mostly about hours of meaningful exposure. Here is what common schedules add up to over a typical school year. These are rough estimates for planning, not promises about results.

| Schedule | Weeks per year (approx.) | Arabic hours per year (approx.) |
|---|---|---|
| 45 minutes once a week | 34 | 25 |
| 1 hour once a week | 34 | 34 |
| 2.5 hours every Saturday | 38 | 95 |
| 1 hour twice a week | 34 | 68 |
| Home practice, 10 minutes a day | 52 | 60 |

Two things stand out. First, a weekly class builds reading and writing steadily, but it rarely builds fluent speaking on its own. Second, 10 minutes a day at home adds as much as a second weekly class. That is why the best programs send home something short and specific to practise.

If your goal is reading, our step-by-step guide on [how to teach kids to read Arabic](/blog/arabic-reading-for-kids) shows what that daily practice can look like.

## How to observe a trial class

Most good schools will let you sit in, or offer a trial session. Spend 20 minutes watching and look for these signs:

- **Who is talking?** In a strong class, children speak and read aloud often. If the teacher talks for most of the session, children are getting little practice.
- **Is Arabic used for real communication?** Greetings, instructions and praise in Arabic are a good sign.
- **Are children reading?** Even beginners should be decoding letters and syllables, not only copying.
- **How are mistakes handled?** Look for calm, quick correction and a second try. Shaming or long lectures shut children down.
- **Is every child involved?** Watch the quiet child in the corner. Does the teacher bring them in?
- **Is there a routine?** Predictable segments (review, new learning, practice, game) help children settle and focus.

Ask your child afterwards what they enjoyed. Enjoyment is not everything, but a child who looks forward to class will attend more consistently, and consistency drives progress.

## Red flags to take seriously

A few warning signs are worth more than a long list of positives:

- No one can tell you what your child will learn this year.
- All children use the same book regardless of level.
- Teachers change often and no one explains why.
- Progress is only ever described as "good, mashaAllah," with no specifics.
- Classes regularly start late or are cancelled without notice.
- Harsh discipline, or children who look anxious rather than engaged.
- Pressure to pay for the full year before you have seen a class.

None of these alone means a program is bad. But two or three together usually mean you will spend the year frustrated.

## Arabic school options in Ottawa

Ottawa families have more choice than many people realize. Here is a quick map of the main routes; our full guide to [Arabic classes for kids in Ottawa](/blog/arabic-classes-for-kids-ottawa) goes deeper.

**School board language programs.** The Ottawa-Carleton District School Board runs an [International and Indigenous Languages elementary program](https://www.ocdsb.ca/programs-learning/continuing-education/international-languages-program/international-indigenous-languages-elementary-program) that lists Arabic for students from JK to Grade 8. For 2026–2027 it offers in-person Saturday morning classes at a number of schools and virtual Saturday-afternoon and after-school options, with a small activity fee for Ontario students. Check the board's page for current locations and registration. The Ottawa Catholic School Board runs its own international languages program, so check its current list too.

**Weekend Islamic schools.** Many mosques and community organizations run Saturday or Sunday programs that combine Arabic with Quran and Islamic studies. Our overview of [weekend Islamic schools in Ottawa](/blog/weekend-islamic-schools-ottawa) explains how to compare them.

**Full-time Islamic schools.** Several schools teach Arabic every day as part of the regular timetable. See [Ottawa's Islamic schools compared](/blog/islamic-schools-in-ottawa-guide) for an overview.

**Online classes and tutors.** These can fill gaps, especially for families outside the city. Our guide to [online Arabic classes for kids](/blog/learn-arabic-online-for-kids) covers how to vet them.

## The bottom line

The right Arabic school for kids is the one that matches your goal, places your child at the right level, gives them plenty of time to read and speak, and keeps you in the loop. Visit, watch a class, ask the 10 questions above, and pair whatever you choose with a few minutes of daily practice at home. That combination, more than any single program, is what turns a child into a confident Arabic reader.
