What is ijazah? An **ijazah** (إجازة) is a written licence from a qualified Quran teacher confirming that a student has recited the Quran to them accurately, usually from beginning to end, and giving the student permission to recite and teach it in turn. It comes with a **sanad**, a chain of named teachers that links the student back, generation by generation, to the Prophet ﷺ.

For parents, the question usually comes up in two ways: “Does my child’s teacher have an ijazah?” and “Should my child aim for one?” This guide answers both, in plain language.

> **Key takeaways**
> - An ijazah is permission and certification from a teacher who holds an ijazah themselves; the sanad is the chain of teachers behind it.
> - The main types are recitation (reading from the Mushaf), memorization (reciting the whole Quran from memory) and qira’at (additional authentic modes of recitation).
> - An ijazah is a strong sign of accuracy, but it is not the only thing that makes a good children’s teacher.
> - Some children and teens do earn an ijazah, but it comes after years of solid reading, tajweed and (for hifz) memorization. It is a destination, not a starting point.
> - Be cautious of programs that promise a fast or paid-for ijazah.

## What is ijazah in the Quran?

The word *ijazah* comes from an Arabic root meaning to permit or to allow. In Quranic studies, an ijazah is the teacher’s formal statement that the student has read to them with correct pronunciation and tajweed, and is now authorized to recite and pass the recitation on to others.

A typical Quran ijazah document includes:

- The student’s name and the teacher’s (the *mujiz*, or “the one who grants permission”) name.
- What was recited: the whole Quran, and whether it was read from the Mushaf or from memory.
- The *riwayah* (transmission) it was recited in, most commonly **Hafs ‘an ‘Asim**, the recitation used in most printed Mushafs today.
- The full sanad, listing the teacher’s teacher, and so on, back to the Prophet ﷺ.
- The teacher’s signature, often with conditions or advice for the student.

In other words, an ijazah is not a grade or a diploma from an institution. It is a personal testimony from one reciter about another.

## What is a sanad, and why does it matter?

**A sanad is the unbroken chain of teachers through which a recitation has been passed on.** Every person in the chain recited to the person before them and was checked.

This mirrors how the Quran was first taught. Allah told the Prophet ﷺ not to rush his recitation while revelation came, but to follow it as it was recited to him:

لَا تُحَرِّكْ بِهِۦ لِسَانَكَ لِتَعْجَلَ بِهِۦٓ ۝ إِنَّ عَلَيْنَا جَمْعَهُۥ وَقُرْءَانَهُۥ ۝ فَإِذَا قَرَأْنَـٰهُ فَٱتَّبِعْ قُرْءَانَهُۥ

“Move not your tongue with it, [O Muḥammad], to hasten with it. Indeed, upon Us is its collection [in your heart] and [to make possible] its recitation. So when We have recited it [through Gabriel], then follow its recitation.” ([Surah Al-Qiyamah 75:16–18](https://quran.com/75/16-18), Sahih International)

The Prophet ﷺ himself reviewed the Quran with Jibril. Abu Hurayrah reported that Jibril used to review the Quran with the Prophet ﷺ once every year, and twice in the year he passed away ([Sahih al-Bukhari 4998](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:4998)). The Companions then learned directly from him: Abdullah ibn Mas‘ud said he learned more than seventy surahs directly from the Messenger of Allah ﷺ ([Sahih al-Bukhari 5000](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:5000)), and the Prophet ﷺ named four Companions, including Ibn Mas‘ud and Ubayy ibn Ka‘b, from whom people should learn the Quran ([Sahih al-Bukhari 3808](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3808)).

### A simplified example of a chain

Take the Hafs ‘an ‘Asim recitation. Today’s student recites to their teacher, whose chain runs back through many generations of reciters to **Hafs** (d. 180 AH), who learned from **‘Asim ibn Abi al-Najud** of Kufa. ‘Asim learned from **Abu ‘Abd al-Rahman al-Sulami**, who took from ‘Ali ibn Abi Talib, and from **Zirr ibn Hubaysh**, who took from Abdullah ibn Mas‘ud. Those Companions learned from the Prophet ﷺ, who received it from Jibril.

A lovely detail: in the chain of the famous hadith “The best among you are those who learn the Quran and teach it” ([Sahih al-Bukhari 5027](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:5027)), the Arabic text records that Abu ‘Abd al-Rahman al-Sulami taught the Quran from the time of ‘Uthman’s caliphate until the era of al-Hajjaj, and said that this hadith was what had seated him in that place. The same teacher who taught ‘Asim spent his life on that one hadith.

Scholars of early Islam valued chains highly. ‘Abdullah ibn al-Mubarak said that the chain of narration is part of the religion, and without it anyone could say whatever they wished ([Sahih Muslim, Introduction](https://sunnah.com/muslim/introduction/31)).

## Types of Quran ijazah

Not every ijazah means the same thing. When a teacher says “I have ijazah”, it is fair and polite to ask, “In what?”

| Type | What the student did | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|
| **Ijazah in recitation** (reading from the Mushaf) | Recited the entire Quran, looking at the Mushaf, with correct tajweed | Strong readers who want to teach accurate recitation |
| **Ijazah in memorization** (recitation from memory) | Recited the entire Quran from memory to the teacher, with tajweed | Huffadh (those who have memorized the Quran) |
| **Ijazah in one riwayah** | Recited in a single transmission, usually Hafs ‘an ‘Asim | The most common Quran ijazah |
| **Ijazah in the qira’at** | Recited in additional authentic modes (for example the seven or ten qira’at), usually after studying classical poems such as al-Shatibiyyah | Advanced students and specialists |
| **Ijazah in a tajweed text** | Studied and recited a classical tajweed poem, such as Tuhfat al-Atfal or al-Muqaddimah al-Jazariyyah | Students of tajweed theory |

Some teachers also give ijazah for a portion, such as a single surah or juz. Scholars of recitation have discussed the conditions for this, so a partial ijazah is best understood as a blessed milestone rather than a full licence to teach the whole Quran.

### Ijazah vs hifz certificate vs “completion” certificate

Many schools and online academies give certificates when a child finishes a qaida, a juz or the whole Quran. These are valuable for motivation, but they are not the same as an ijazah with a sanad. A **hifz completion certificate** says the student memorized the Quran in that program. An **ijazah** says a specific teacher with a chain heard the student and authorized them.

## Does a Quran teacher need an ijazah?

**No, an ijazah is not strictly required to teach a child to read the Quran, but it is one of the clearest signs that a teacher’s recitation has been carefully checked.** Many excellent teachers of young children teach the alphabet, qaida and short surahs accurately without holding a full ijazah, because they studied under qualified teachers for years.

For a parent, think of it this way:

- For **early reading and short surahs**, look for accurate pronunciation, patience and real skill with children.
- For **tajweed correction and serious hifz**, a teacher with an ijazah (or who is supervised by one) is a significant advantage.
- For **qira’at or granting ijazah**, the teacher must hold an ijazah in that very thing.

An ijazah also says nothing about whether someone is good with a nervous six-year-old, communicates with parents, or follows safe practices. Our guide to [finding a Quran tutor in Ottawa](/blog/quran-tutor-ottawa) covers the rest of the vetting.

### How to ask about a teacher’s ijazah politely

1. Ask what they hold an ijazah in (recitation, memorization, riwayah, qira’at).
2. Ask who their teacher was. Reputable teachers are happy to name their mujiz.
3. Ask whether they can show the document; many keep a copy.
4. Ask how they teach children, how they correct mistakes and how they report progress.

## Can a child earn an ijazah?

Yes, some do. Children and teens who have finished memorizing the Quran with strong tajweed sometimes go on to recite the whole Quran to a mujiz. But it is demanding: the student must recite every page accurately, often over many sessions, and the teacher may stop them repeatedly to correct details.

In practice, the path looks like this:

1. **Reading fluency.** Your child learns the letters, the qaida and joined reading. See [how to teach a child to read Quran](/blog/quran-reading-for-beginners-kids).
2. **Tajweed foundations.** Correct articulation points and core rules; our guide to [tajweed rules for kids](/blog/tajweed-rules-for-kids) explains them simply.
3. **Memorization (for an ijazah from memory).** Steady hifz with strong revision; strong revision matters more than speed.
4. **Polishing.** A teacher with ijazah checks the recitation thoroughly, often over months.
5. **Full recitation to the mujiz.** The student recites the entire Quran, and the teacher grants the ijazah when satisfied.

Requirements, including any minimum age, are set by the individual teacher or institution, so ask directly.

### How long does it take to get an ijazah?

There is no fixed timeline. For a fluent reader, completing a full recitation to a teacher might take months; for a hafiz, polishing their memorization to ijazah standard can take longer, depending on how often they meet their teacher and how strong their revision is. Be wary of any program that promises a full ijazah in a few weeks.

## Is an ijazah worth pursuing?

An ijazah is a beautiful goal. It connects a young person to an unbroken chain of reciters and gives them a share in teaching the Quran, which the Prophet ﷺ praised in [Sahih al-Bukhari 5027](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:5027). It also builds a rare kind of discipline.

But it should never become a status symbol. Keep these principles in mind:

- **Intention first.** The goal is to recite Allah’s words correctly and sincerely, not to collect a certificate.
- **Accuracy over speed.** A child who reads slowly and correctly is on a better path than one rushed toward a paper.
- **No pressure.** Many devoted Muslims never hold an ijazah and still recite beautifully. The Prophet ﷺ promised a double reward to the one who recites with difficulty ([Sahih al-Bukhari 4937](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:4937)).

### Red flags to watch for

- An “ijazah” issued after a short course with no full recitation.
- Pay-to-receive certificates where the fee buys the document rather than teaching time.
- A teacher who will not name their own teacher or chain.
- Pressure on young children to “finish fast” for a ceremony.
