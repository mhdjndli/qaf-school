Kids who speak Arabic at home but can't read it are extremely common in Canada, and they are not behind. They are heritage speakers: children with real listening and speaking skills in a family dialect, who have never been taught to decode the Arabic script or to work in Fusha, the standard Arabic used in books, school and the Quran. With the right approach, they usually learn to read faster than complete beginners, because they already know the sounds and thousands of words.

This guide explains why it happens, what your child already has, how to teach a heritage speaker to read Arabic step by step, how to bridge dialect and Fusha, and which Arabic reading options exist for families in Ottawa.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Speaking Arabic at home and reading Arabic are different skills; children need to be taught the script and Fusha explicitly.
> - Heritage speakers have a big head start: they know the sounds, rhythm and many roots, so placement in a beginner class can bore them.
> - Teach reading in short daily sessions, starting with letters and vowel marks, then words your child already knows.
> - Expect dialect "spelling" mistakes such as writing ت for ث; they show your child is connecting speech to print.
> - In Ottawa, options include free or low-cost school board Arabic classes on Saturdays, weekend Islamic schools and private tutors.

## Kids who speak Arabic at home but can't read: why it happens

If your child chats with grandparents in Arabic but cannot read a street sign in Arabic, several things are usually at work.

**Arabic has two layers.** Arabic is diglossic: families speak a regional dialect (Levantine, Egyptian, Iraqi, Gulf, Maghrebi and others), while reading and writing use Fusha (Modern Standard Arabic, close to Classical Arabic). A child can be fluent in the first and have little exposure to the second.

**Reading needs to be taught.** No child picks up reading by listening, in any language. English reading is taught for hours every week at school; Arabic reading, for most Canadian children, is taught for a few hours a week at most, or not at all.

**English takes over at school age.** Once children start JK, English becomes the language of friends, school and screens. Many children keep understanding Arabic but begin answering in English, and their Arabic vocabulary stops growing with their age.

**Quran reading is not the same as general reading.** Some children read the Quran with full vowel marks (tashkeel) but cannot read a children's story without them, or read fluently without understanding. Both are common and both can be built on.

The good news is that the gap is in literacy, not in language. That is a much easier gap to close.

## What heritage speakers already have and what they need

Heritage learners bring a different profile from beginners. The [National Heritage Language Resource Center](https://nhlrc.ucla.edu/nhlrc/about) at UCLA describes heritage speakers as bilinguals whose skills differ from monolingual speakers, and sees those skills as a foundation to build literacy on rather than a deficit.

| Usually strong | Usually needs work |
|---|---|
| Hearing and producing Arabic sounds, including ح, ع, خ | Recognizing letter shapes in all four positions |
| Natural rhythm and intonation | Vowel marks (fatha, kasra, damma, sukun, shadda) |
| Everyday vocabulary: family, food, home, feelings | Fusha vocabulary for school topics and stories |
| Understanding conversations | Spelling sounds the dialect merges or changes |
| Confidence speaking with family | Reading fluency and stamina |
| Familiarity with Islamic phrases | Writing letters and joining them correctly |

Knowing this profile helps you, and any teacher, skip what your child already has and focus on what they actually need.

## Heritage learner or beginner: why placement matters

**A heritage learner is a child who grew up hearing or speaking a language at home but has not been formally schooled in it.** A beginner has no home exposure. Putting a heritage speaker in a pure-beginner class often backfires: they are bored during speaking activities and still lost when reading starts, which they read as "Arabic class is not for me".

| | Beginner | Heritage speaker |
|---|---|---|
| Listening | Starts from zero | Strong in dialect |
| Speaking | Starts from zero | Comfortable, mostly dialect |
| Reading | Starts from zero | Starts from zero or very little |
| Best class focus | Vocabulary, sounds, script together | Script, vowel marks, Fusha, reading fluency |
| Common risk | Overload | Boredom, then embarrassment |

When choosing a program, ask directly: "How do you place children who speak Arabic at home but can't read?" A good program will assess reading and speaking separately. Our overview of [Arabic classes for kids in Ottawa](/blog/arabic-classes-for-kids-ottawa) lists local formats.

## How to teach a heritage speaker to read Arabic, step by step

You do not need to be a teacher to support this at home. Ten to fifteen minutes a day, most days, is more effective than one long session on the weekend.

### Step 1: Check what your child can already do

Show your child the letters, a few short words with vowel marks, and a short sentence. Note what they recognise. Some children already know half the alphabet from Quran class; others know none.

### Step 2: Teach letters through sounds they already make

Heritage speakers already say ع and ح correctly, which beginners struggle with for months. Connect each letter to a word your child uses every day: ب for بيت, م for ماما, ح for حليب. Our guide to [teaching the Arabic alphabet to kids](/blog/teach-arabic-alphabet-to-kids) covers letter order and shapes.

### Step 3: Add the short vowels early

Children read Arabic accurately much sooner when they learn fatha, kasra and damma from the start, then sukun and shadda. Practise syllables (بَ بِ بُ) until they are automatic.

### Step 4: Read words they already know

This is the heritage speaker's superpower. Read words from your family's life: names of relatives, foods, toys. Recognising a word they say every day is deeply motivating, because reading suddenly "unlocks" their own language.

### Step 5: Move to short Fusha texts

Choose simple vowelled readers or picture books with short sentences. When a Fusha word differs from your dialect, say both: "In the book it says ماذا; at home we say شو or إيش." Our list of [Arabic books for kids](/blog/arabic-books-for-kids) suggests levelled options.

### Step 6: Write a little every day

Copying a word or a short sentence each day locks in letter shapes and joining rules. Keep it to a few lines; handwriting fatigue kills motivation.

### Step 7: Build stamina and fluency

Once your child can decode, reread the same short text several times across a week. Fluency comes from repetition, not from constantly new material.

> **Tip:** Keep speaking your dialect at home. Reading lessons should add Fusha, not replace the family language your child already loves.

## Bridging dialect and Fusha

Heritage speakers sometimes feel that "book Arabic" is a foreign language. Show them how close the two are: most roots are shared, and many everyday words are identical. Treat Fusha as a "dressed-up" version of the Arabic they already speak.

There is also a spiritual bridge. Allah says:

إِنَّآ أَنزَلْنَـٰهُ قُرْءَٰنًا عَرَبِيًّا لَّعَلَّكُمْ تَعْقِلُونَ

"Indeed, We have sent it down as an Arabic Qur'an that you might understand." ([Surah Yusuf 12:2](https://quran.com/12/2))

For a heritage speaker, learning to read Fusha is the path to understanding the Quran directly, not just reciting it. For a deeper look at which variety to prioritise, see our guide to [Fusha vs dialect for kids](/blog/fusha-vs-dialect-for-kids).

## Common challenges and how to handle them

**Spelling the way they speak.** Many dialects pronounce ث as ت or س, ذ as د or ز, and ق as a glottal stop or a "g". Children then spell words the way they hear them at home. Treat this as a sign of good progress: they are mapping speech to print. Teach the Fusha sound and spelling side by side.

**Embarrassment.** Children who speak well can feel ashamed that they read like a younger child. Praise their speaking openly, and make reading practice private and short until confidence grows.

**Answering in English.** Very common after age 5. Do not punish it. Keep using Arabic yourself, create Arabic-only moments (meals, car rides, calls with grandparents), and give reading a real purpose, such as reading a card from Teta.

**Mixed dialects at home.** If parents speak different dialects, that is a strength, not confusion. Fusha becomes the shared "school" Arabic for the whole family. Our post on [raising trilingual kids in Canada](/blog/raising-trilingual-kids-canada) has more on balancing languages.

**Apps that are too easy.** Many apps are built for complete beginners. Use them for letter practice only, and choose ones with Fusha audio and vowel marks.

## Arabic reading options for heritage learners in Ottawa

Ottawa has more options than many families realise.

**School board Saturday classes.** The OCDSB's [International and Indigenous Languages Elementary Program](https://ocdsb.ca/programs-learning/continuing-education/international-languages-program/international-indigenous-languages-elementary-program) lists Arabic for 2026–2027, open to students from JK to Grade 8 from all school boards, with no prior knowledge required. In-person classes run Saturday mornings from 9:30 AM to 12:00 PM at a number of schools across the city, with virtual Saturday afternoon and weekday after-school options. The listed in-person fee is a $20 activity fee for Ontario residents; check the page for current details, locations and registration.

**High school credits.** The OCDSB also lists [Arabic credit courses for secondary students](https://ocdsb.ca/programs-learning/continuing-education/international-languages-program/international-languages-secondary-program2), held virtually on weekday evenings, which can suit teens who want to formalise their skills.

**Catholic board programs.** The OCSB runs [international and Indigenous language programs](https://www.ocsb.ca/our-schools/continuing-and-community-education/) for elementary-age children; contact the board to ask which languages are offered this year.

**Weekend Islamic schools and masjid programs.** These usually combine Arabic reading with Quran and Islamic studies. Compare them in our guide to [weekend Islamic schools in Ottawa](/blog/weekend-islamic-schools-ottawa).

**QAF Sunday Camp.** For ages 8–15, the [Sunday Quran and Arabic camp in Ottawa](/sunday-camp) runs Sundays from 11 AM to 3 PM at 900 Dynes Rd in Nepean, with Arabic reading and writing alongside Quran, math and STEM, and soccer, in junior and senior classes.

## Turn their spoken Arabic into a reading life

A child who speaks Arabic at home already owns the hardest part of the language. What they need now is the key to the script and a gentle bridge to Fusha: a few minutes of reading a day, words from their own life, and a class that recognises what they bring. Start this week, and within months they can be reading their first Arabic story, and in time, understanding the Quran in its own language.
