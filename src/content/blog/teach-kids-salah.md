If you're wondering how to teach kids salah, the answer is stages: toddlers copy you, five- and six-year-olds learn the positions and short words, and from age seven children are gently asked to pray regularly, as the Prophet ﷺ instructed. Love comes first, accuracy second. This guide gives you the hadith, an age-by-age plan, a kid-friendly walkthrough of wudu and salah, and the tools that actually help.

> **Key takeaways**
> - The Prophet ﷺ said to command children to pray at **seven** (Sunan Abi Dawud 495). Salah only becomes obligatory at puberty, so the years before are for practice.
> - Ages 2–4 learn by imitation, ages 5–6 learn the positions and short words, and from 7 children build towards regular prayers.
> - Teach wudu and salah one step at a time, the positions first and the words after.
> - Children who link salah with warmth, praise and family time tend to keep praying as they grow.
> - Details such as where to place the hands differ slightly between the four madhhabs. Follow what your family and local masjid practise.

## What the hadith says about age seven

The key narration comes from Abdullah ibn Amr ibn al-As. The Messenger of Allah ﷺ said:

مُرُوا أَوْلاَدَكُمْ بِالصَّلاَةِ وَهُمْ أَبْنَاءُ سَبْعِ سِنِينَ وَاضْرِبُوهُمْ عَلَيْهَا وَهُمْ أَبْنَاءُ عَشْرِ سِنِينَ وَفَرِّقُوا بَيْنَهُمْ فِي الْمَضَاجِعِ

"Command your children to pray when they become seven years old, and beat them for it (prayer) when they become ten years old; and arrange their beds (to sleep) separately." ([Sunan Abi Dawud 495](https://sunnah.com/abudawud:495), graded hasan sahih by al-Albani; a similar narration from Sabrah is at Sunan Abi Dawud 494.)

Three points help parents read this hadith properly:

- **Seven is when you start asking, not when learning starts.** Scholars explain that encouragement should begin much earlier. A SeekersGuidance answer on [when children should start praying](https://seekersguidance.org/answers/hanafi-fiqh/when-should-children-start-praying/) notes that parents should encourage prayer "from a young age" and reward it.
- **Salah is not yet obligatory on the child.** The Prophet ﷺ said that a boy's actions are not recorded "till he reaches puberty" (Sunan Abi Dawud 4403). The years from seven to puberty are training years.
- **The age-ten instruction is about discipline, not harshness.** Classical scholars limited it to a light, non-injuring measure as a last resort, never on the face. The Prophet ﷺ himself never struck anyone with his hand, "neither a woman nor a servant" ([Sahih Muslim 2328a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:2328a)). For a kindergartener the hadith's real lesson is the first part: a calm, steady instruction to pray, given with love.

The Quran puts the same duty on parents: "And enjoin prayer upon your family and be steadfast therein" (Surah Ta-Ha 20:132). Luqman's advice to his son also begins with salah: يَـٰبُنَىَّ أَقِمِ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ, "O my son, establish prayer" (Surah Luqman 31:17).

## Salah by age: what to expect

There is no exam at seven. Think of salah as a skill your child grows into over several years.

| Age | Main goal | What it looks like | Parent's role |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2–4 | Imitation and belonging | Stands beside you, copies ruku and sujud, says "Allahu Akbar" | Pray where they can see you; welcome them on the mat |
| 5–6 | Learning the positions and short words | Knows the order of movements, says Al-Fatihah with help, learns wudu | Teach one step at a time; pray one salah together daily |
| 7–9 | Regular practice | Prays some daily prayers on their own, builds up to five | Remind gently at prayer times; track progress on a chart |
| 10+ | Consistency and understanding | Prays all five with little help, knows meanings | Hold them to it kindly; talk about why we pray |

### Ages 2–4: imitation

Toddlers learn salah by watching. If they climb on your back during sujud, you are in good company: the Prophet ﷺ once made a sujud so long that the Companions worried, and then explained, "my son was riding on my back and I did not like to disturb him until he had enough" (Sunan an-Nasa'i 1141). He also prayed while carrying his granddaughter Umamah, putting her down for sujud and picking her up again when he stood (Sahih al-Bukhari 516).

At this age, aim for presence, not performance. A child-sized mat beside yours and a few "Allahu Akbar"s are a real success.

### Ages 5–6: positions and short words

This is the sweet spot for structured learning. Children this age love sequences, songs and "big kid" jobs. Teach the movements first, then add the words a phrase at a time. Al-Fatihah and one short surah such as Al-Ikhlas are enough to pray. Our guide to [short surahs for kids](/blog/short-surahs-for-kids) suggests a memorisation order.

In our kindergarten classroom we've found that five-year-olds learn fastest when the steps are visual (picture cards on the wall) and physical (doing each position slowly together), and when they are allowed to be imperfect.

### Ages 7 and up: regular prayer

From seven, follow the hadith: tell your child clearly that it is time to pray, and keep telling them, kindly, every day. Many families start with one or two prayers a day, often Maghrib and Isha when everyone is home, and add more over the following months. By ten, most children can manage all five with reminders.

## How to teach wudu step by step

Wudu (ablution) comes before salah, and children usually enjoy it because it involves water. The essentials are in the Quran (Surah Al-Ma'idah [5:6](https://quran.com/5/6)): washing the face, the arms to the elbows, wiping the head and washing the feet to the ankles. The Sunnah adds the rest, as Uthman ibn Affan demonstrated it (Sahih al-Bukhari 159).

1. **Step 1: Make the intention** in your heart and say "Bismillah."
2. **Step 2: Wash both hands** up to the wrists, three times.
3. **Step 3: Rinse the mouth** three times.
4. **Step 4: Rinse the nose** by sniffing water in gently and blowing it out, three times.
5. **Step 5: Wash the face** from hairline to chin and ear to ear, three times.
6. **Step 6: Wash the arms** up to and including the elbows, right first, three times each.
7. **Step 7: Wipe the head** once with wet hands (most schools also wipe the ears).
8. **Step 8: Wash the feet** up to and including the ankles, right first, three times each.

Afterwards, teach your child the testimony the Prophet ﷺ recommended after wudu (Sahih Muslim 234a):

أَشْهَدُ أَنْ لاَ إِلَهَ إِلاَّ اللَّهُ وَأَنَّ مُحَمَّدًا عَبْدُ اللَّهِ وَرَسُولُهُ

*Ash-hadu an la ilaha illallah, wa anna Muhammadan abdullahi wa rasuluh.* "I testify that there is no god but Allah, and that Muhammad is the servant of Allah and His Messenger."

> **Tip:** A step stool at the sink, a small towel of their own and warm water in winter make wudu something children look forward to, not something they put off. Teach one new step a day for a week.

## How to teach kids salah step by step

The Prophet ﷺ said, "Pray as you have seen me praying" ([Sahih al-Bukhari 631](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:631)). Children learn exactly this way: by seeing you, then copying you. Here is a simple walkthrough of a two-rak'ah prayer, such as Fajr, in child-friendly language.

1. **Step 1: Get ready.** Make wudu, wear clean clothes, and stand on a clean mat facing the qiblah.
2. **Step 2: Intend.** Think in your heart, "I am praying Fajr for Allah." The intention does not need to be said out loud.
3. **Step 3: Say "Allahu Akbar"** (Allah is the Greatest) and raise your hands. Now you are in salah, talking to Allah.
4. **Step 4: Stand and recite Al-Fatihah** ([Surah 1](https://quran.com/1)), then a short surah such as Al-Ikhlas.
5. **Step 5: Bow (ruku).** Say "Allahu Akbar," bend with your hands on your knees and back flat, and say *Subhana Rabbiyal-Azim* (Glory be to my Lord, the Most Great), usually three times.
6. **Step 6: Stand up.** Say *Sami'allahu liman hamidah* (Allah hears the one who praises Him), then *Rabbana wa lakal-hamd* (Our Lord, all praise is Yours).
7. **Step 7: Prostrate (sujud).** Say "Allahu Akbar" and put your forehead, nose, hands, knees and toes on the floor. Say *Subhana Rabbiyal-A'la* (Glory be to my Lord, the Most High), usually three times. The wording for ruku and sujud is in Sahih Muslim 772.
8. **Step 8: Sit up, then prostrate again.** Say "Allahu Akbar" each time you move.
9. **Step 9: Stand for the second rak'ah** and repeat steps 4 to 8.
10. **Step 10: Sit for the tashahhud.** Older children recite *at-Tahiyyatu lillah...* (Sahih al-Bukhari 831) and the salawat on the Prophet ﷺ. Younger ones can sit quietly while you recite.
11. **Step 11: End with salam.** Turn your head to the right, saying *As-salamu alaykum wa rahmatullah*, then to the left.

### Al-Fatihah with transliteration

بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ ۝ ٱلْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ رَبِّ ٱلْعَـٰلَمِينَ ۝ ٱلرَّحْمَـٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ ۝ مَـٰلِكِ يَوْمِ ٱلدِّينِ ۝ إِيَّاكَ نَعْبُدُ وَإِيَّاكَ نَسْتَعِينُ ۝ ٱهْدِنَا ٱلصِّرَٰطَ ٱلْمُسْتَقِيمَ ۝ صِرَٰطَ ٱلَّذِينَ أَنْعَمْتَ عَلَيْهِمْ غَيْرِ ٱلْمَغْضُوبِ عَلَيْهِمْ وَلَا ٱلضَّآلِّينَ

*Bismillahir-Rahmanir-Rahim. Al-hamdu lillahi Rabbil-alamin. Ar-Rahmanir-Rahim. Maliki yawmid-din. Iyyaka na'budu wa iyyaka nasta'in. Ihdinas-siratal-mustaqim. Siratal-ladhina an'amta alayhim, ghayril-maghdubi alayhim wa lad-dallin.*

Transliteration is a bridge, not the destination. Let your child hear Al-Fatihah recited correctly every day (yours, a teacher's, or a reciter on Quran.com) so their pronunciation grows from listening.

### How many rak'ahs in each prayer?

| Prayer | Fard rak'ahs | Good first goal for a 7-year-old |
|---|---|---|
| Fajr | 2 | Pray it on weekends with a parent |
| Dhuhr | 4 | Pray it on weekends and holidays |
| Asr | 4 | Pray it after school |
| Maghrib | 3 | Pray it together as a family |
| Isha | 4 | Pray it before bed (in summer, earlier with a parent's guidance on timing) |

### A note on madhhab differences

The core of salah is shared by all Muslims, but small details vary between the Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali schools: where the hands rest while standing, whether they are raised around ruku, how one sits in tashahhud, and whether "Ameen" is said aloud. All four are valid, mainstream schools. Teach your child the way your family prays. If they notice a friend praying slightly differently, explain that scholars studied the Sunnah carefully and reached more than one sound conclusion.

## Tools that make learning salah easier

You don't need to buy anything to teach salah, but a few tools make it concrete for young children.

- **A child's own prayer mat.** Ownership matters. Let them choose it, and keep it rolled up next to yours. Interactive mats that show the positions, such as My Salah Mat, are popular with five- to eight-year-olds.
- **A prayer chart.** A simple grid of five prayers by seven days, with a sticker for each one prayed. Celebrate effort, not perfection. Avoid comparing siblings' charts.
- **Picture cards of each position.** Print or draw takbir, qiyam, ruku, sujud and sitting. Mix them up and let your child put them in order. Our list of [the best Islamic books for kids](/blog/best-islamic-books-for-kids) includes picture-book guides to salah.
- **A prayer-time routine.** Use the adhan from a masjid app as the household signal: screens off, wudu, mat. Predictable cues reduce arguments.
- **Prayer clothes.** A special thobe, kufi or a child-sized prayer outfit and scarf for girls can make salah feel like an occasion. Treat it as a fun choice, not a requirement, for young children.

## Making salah loved, not feared

How a child feels about salah at six shapes whether they pray at sixteen. The Prophet ﷺ said, "the most beloved deed to Allah is the most regular and constant even if it were little" (Sahih al-Bukhari 6464). Small, consistent and happy beats long, forced and tearful.

- **Pray where they can see you.** Your own consistency is the strongest lesson.
- **Keep it short for little ones.** The Prophet ﷺ shortened his prayer when he heard a child crying "as I dislike to trouble the child's mother" (Sahih al-Bukhari 707). Gentleness with children was part of his salah.
- **Praise specifically.** "I loved how slowly you did your sujud" teaches far more than "good job."
- **Correct gently and later.** Fix one thing at a time, after the prayer, not mid-sujud.
- **Connect salah to Allah's love.** Children need to know who they are praying to. Our guide on [teaching kids about Allah](/blog/teaching-kids-about-allah) has ideas for this.
- **Never use salah as a punishment.** "Go and pray because you were naughty" teaches the wrong lesson.
- **Make dua for them out loud.** Ibrahim's dua is perfect: رَبِّ ٱجْعَلْنِى مُقِيمَ ٱلصَّلَوٰةِ وَمِن ذُرِّيَّتِى, "My Lord, make me an establisher of prayer, and from my descendants" (Surah Ibrahim 14:40).

For more on the gentle, mercy-first approach the Prophet ﷺ showed children, see our [prophetic parenting tips](/blog/prophetic-parenting-tips).

## Common challenges and what to do

**"My child rushes through it."** Slow down together. Count three breaths in sujud. Rushing usually means the child doesn't yet know what the words mean, so teach the meanings of Al-Fatihah one verse at a time.

**"They refuse to pray."** Stay calm and stay consistent. Offer choices within the expectation ("Do you want to pray on the blue mat or the green one?"). Pray with them instead of sending them.

**"They forget the steps."** Totally normal before seven. Pray beside them and let them follow your movements. Picture cards help.

**"Fajr is too hard."** For young children, it is fine to start with weekend Fajr. Build the habit slowly.

## An Ottawa angle: prayer times through the seasons

Families in Ottawa and across Canada face a challenge that families nearer the equator don't: prayer times swing widely through the year. In December, Maghrib comes before 4:30 PM, so Asr and Maghrib can both fall in the after-school window. In June, Isha can be well after 10 PM, long after a five-year-old's bedtime.

Use this to your advantage: winter weekends are ideal for building the Dhuhr-Asr-Maghrib habit, and young children are not yet obligated, so there is no need to keep a five-year-old up for a late summer Isha.

## Bringing it all together

Teaching kids salah is a long, gentle journey: imitation at two, positions at five, regular prayer from seven, and real ownership by ten. Teach wudu first, then the movements, then the words, and keep every step warm. Your child does not need to pray perfectly. They need to see you pray, feel welcome on the mat and hear, again and again, that Allah loves them.
