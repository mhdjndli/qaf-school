Part time hifz means memorizing the Quran while your child keeps attending regular school, usually through a few evening or weekend sessions a week plus short daily revision at home. It is slower than full-time hifz, but it is the path most Muslim children in Canada actually take, and with the right routine it works. This guide covers how part-time hifz compares with full-time, sample weekly schedules by age, a step-by-step setup plan and the warning signs that a child is overloaded.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Part time hifz fits around school by keeping new lessons small and revision daily. Consistency matters far more than the size of each lesson.
> - Most part-time students need 3–5 sessions with a teacher or parent each week, plus 15–30 minutes of daily home revision.
> - Protect sleep, homework and play. A child who is exhausted will not retain what they memorize.
> - Use holidays, summers and long weekends for extra revision rather than racing ahead with new pages.
> - Choose a program that reports progress clearly, so you can support revision at home.

## What is part time hifz?

**Part time hifz is a structured Quran memorization plan that runs alongside a child’s regular schooling, rather than replacing it.** The child attends public, Catholic, private or Islamic school during the day and memorizes in the evenings, early mornings or on weekends, with a teacher hearing new lessons and checking revision several times a week.

Full-time hifz is different: a student spends most of the school day on memorization, often for one to three years, and catches up on academics alongside or afterwards. Both lead to the same goal. Part-time simply spreads the work over more years.

The Prophet ﷺ was asked which deeds Allah loves most, and he said: “The most regular constant deeds even though they may be few,” adding, “Don’t take upon yourselves, except the deeds which are within your ability” ([Sahih al-Bukhari 6465](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:6465)). That is the whole philosophy of part-time hifz in one hadith.

## Part time vs full time hifz at a glance

| | Part time hifz | Full time hifz |
|---|---|---|
| **Hours per week** | About 4–10, including home revision | About 20–30 or more |
| **Typical new lesson** | A few lines to half a page a day | Often a page or more a day |
| **School** | Continues as normal | Paused, reduced or combined with hifz |
| **Typical timeline** | Several years, depending on pace | Often 1–3 years for committed students |
| **Main challenge** | Fitting revision into busy weeks | Intensity, fatigue and academic catch-up |
| **Best for** | Most children, especially ages 7–15 in regular school | Highly motivated students with strong family support |

Neither is better for every child. Part-time hifz keeps school, friends and activities in place, and many children stay more motivated because hifz is one part of a balanced life rather than all of it. For a fuller picture of paces and years, read [how long it takes to memorize the Quran](/blog/how-long-does-hifz-take).

## Can a child really do hifz while attending regular school?

Yes. Many huffaz memorize the Quran alongside school or work. When [CBC Ottawa interviewed local huffaz](https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/ottawa/memorizing-qur-an-ottawa-hafiz-1.6799786) in 2023, one Ottawa imam described starting hifz at 18 and taking about ten years while juggling school, work and family. It was slow, and he finished.

What makes part-time hifz succeed is not talent but three things:

- **A fixed time slot** that does not move every week.
- **A teacher who hears every new lesson** and corrects pronunciation.
- **A parent who knows what to revise** and makes sure it happens.

Where part-time hifz fails, it is usually because one of these is missing: lessons happen “when there is time,” nobody checks revision, or the new lesson is bigger than the child can hold.

## Sample part time hifz schedules by age

These are realistic starting points for children in a regular school week. Adjust them with your child’s teacher.

### Ages 7–9: short and joyful

| Day | What happens | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Mon–Thu | Home: revise yesterday’s lines, then 2–3 new lines with a parent or teacher | 15–20 min |
| Friday | Light day: recite the week’s lines to a grandparent or sibling | 10 min |
| Saturday or Sunday | Class or halaqah: recite to the teacher, receive next week’s portion | 1–2 hours |

At this age, many children are still finishing Juz Amma. Keep the new lesson tiny and the praise generous.

### Ages 10–12: steady structure

| Day | What happens | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Mon–Thu | Evening class or tutor (2–3 days) or home session: new lesson, recent revision, older revision | 30–45 min |
| Friday | Older revision only | 20 min |
| Weekend | Class or halaqah, plus one longer revision session | 1–3 hours |

Children this age can usually handle a quarter to half a page of new memorization a day, if revision keeps pace.

### Ages 13–15: more independence

| Day | What happens | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Before school | Older revision while the mind is fresh | 15–20 min |
| After Maghrib | New lesson and recent revision, or class | 30–45 min |
| Weekend | Teacher session plus a larger revision block | 2–3 hours |

Teens can manage their own tracking, but they still need a teacher to listen. Exam weeks need a lighter plan, which we cover below.

> **Tip:** Canada’s 24-Hour Movement Guidelines recommend 9–11 hours of sleep a night for ages 5–13 and 8–10 hours for ages 14–17 ([CSEP](https://csepguidelines.ca/guidelines/children-youth/)). If hifz is cutting into sleep, shrink the lesson, not the bedtime.

## How to set up part time hifz: step by step

**Step 1: Agree on the goal with a teacher.** Is the aim the full Quran, or a first milestone such as Juz Amma or Juz Tabarak? A clear milestone keeps motivation high.

**Step 2: Get an honest assessment.** A teacher should check your child’s reading, tajweed and current memorization before setting a pace. A child who still reads slowly may need a few months of reading work first.

**Step 3: Choose a format you can sustain.** Evening classes, a weekend program, a tutor or a mix. If you are in Ottawa, our guide to [hifz programs in Ottawa](/blog/hifz-program-ottawa) compares the local options.

**Step 4: Anchor hifz to a daily event.** After Fajr, after school snack, or after Maghrib. Tying it to an existing habit removes daily negotiation.

**Step 5: Set a daily minimum.** Decide what happens even on the busiest day, such as reciting today’s revision page once. A minimum keeps the chain unbroken.

**Step 6: Split new and old.** Each session should include the new lesson (sabaq), recent revision (sabqi) and older revision (manzil). Our [hifz revision schedule](/blog/hifz-revision-schedule) explains how to divide the time as your child memorizes more.

**Step 7: Track it.** Write down what was memorized and revised, and how strong it was. A simple notebook works, or see our [hifz progress tracker](/blog/hifz-progress-tracker) templates.

**Step 8: Review the plan every term.** In September, January and June, ask the teacher whether the pace is right. Slow down if revision is slipping.

## Balancing school, holidays and hifz

Part time hifz competes with homework, sports, family time and rest. A few principles help:

- **School comes first on school nights, hifz comes first every morning.** Many families do older revision before school and new lessons after homework.
- **Keep one activity, not five.** A child doing hifz plus soccer plus swimming plus piano will drop something. Decide together what matters most this year.
- **Use the car.** Audio of the current juz on the drive to school or practice is free revision time.
- **Plan for exam seasons.** In the weeks before major tests, pause new lessons and keep only revision. Your child will not lose ground, and school stays on track.

### Using holidays and summers wisely

School breaks are where part-time hifz students can make real progress. Winter break, March Break and summer give extra hours without school pressure.

The smartest use of that time is usually **consolidation, not speed**: revising every juz memorized so far until it is strong, fixing recurring mistakes and reciting longer portions to the teacher. Some families add a few extra new pages during summer, but only once revision is solid.

The Prophet ﷺ warned that the Quran escapes the heart faster than camels from their hobbles if it is not refreshed ([Sahih al-Bukhari 5033](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:5033)). Holidays are the perfect time to tie the camels tightly.

## Warning signs your child is overloaded

Watch for these and adjust early:

1. **Tears or stalling** before every session, not just occasional reluctance.
2. **Revision failing** while new lessons continue. This is the most common sign the pace is too fast.
3. **Sleep slipping** because hifz pushes bedtime later.
4. **Schoolwork dropping** noticeably.
5. **Loss of love for the Quran**, such as never reciting voluntarily anymore.

If you see two or more, reduce the new lesson, keep revision, and talk to the teacher. Allah says:

لَا يُكَلِّفُ ٱللَّهُ نَفْسًا إِلَّا وُسْعَهَا

“Allah does not charge a soul except [with that within] its capacity.” (from [Surah Al-Baqarah 2:286](https://quran.com/2/286))

Slowing down is not failure. A child who memorizes slowly and keeps loving the Quran is succeeding. If motivation is the main issue, talk to the teacher about smaller goals and more celebration of milestones.
