The best Arabic songs for kids are short, repetitive and sung clearly: alphabet songs, number and colour songs, and classic nursery songs that Arab families have passed down for generations. For learning, start with Modern Standard Arabic channels like Adam Wa Mishmish and Iftah Ya Simsim, then add a few beloved classics and faith-based nasheeds. Below are 10 verified picks, a quick comparison table, and a simple way to turn listening into real Arabic.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Songs give children a native-speaker pronunciation model, which matters most when parents are still learning Arabic themselves.
> - Fusha (Modern Standard Arabic) channels build school and reading vocabulary; dialect songs build warmth and connection with family.
> - Muslim families hold different views on musical instruments. If that matters to you, choose vocal-only nasheeds, or sing the songs yourself.
> - Listening alone is not enough. Sing along, add actions and use the song's words during the day.
> - Preview any channel before letting a child watch alone, and keep screen time short for under-5s.

## Why Arabic songs work so well for kids

Songs slow language down, repeat it, and attach it to rhythm and movement. A child who can't yet say "أَرْنَب" (rabbit) in conversation will happily sing it ten times in a row.

For parents who don't speak Arabic fluently, songs do something else: they give your child an accurate pronunciation model. Letters like ع, ح and ق are hard to learn from a page, but easy to absorb from a singer.

Songs also carry culture. The same nursery songs your child learns in Ottawa are sung by grandparents in Cairo, Amman and Beirut, which gives children an instant connection with family.

## Fusha or dialect: which Arabic songs should kids learn?

Arabic children's songs come in two kinds of Arabic:

- **Fusha (Modern Standard Arabic)** is the Arabic of books, school and news, and it is close to the Arabic of the Quran. Learning channels such as Adam Wa Mishmish and Iftah Ya Simsim use it.
- **Dialects** (Egyptian, Levantine, Gulf, Maghrebi) are what families speak at home. Most classic nursery songs are in dialect.

Both have a place. If your goal is reading, school or Quran-related literacy, make fusha songs the core. If grandparents speak a dialect, their songs are a gift worth keeping. For a deeper look, see our guide to [fusha vs dialect for kids](/blog/fusha-vs-dialect-for-kids).

## The 10 best Arabic songs and channels for kids

Each pick follows the same structure: what it is, who it's best for, the Arabic it uses and our take.

### 1. Adam Wa Mishmish (آدم ومشمش)

**What it is:** An Arabic music cartoon about a boy named Adam and his cat Mishmish. It began as a family YouTube project when the founders' young son preferred English songs to Arabic ones, and it grew into a channel, books and an app. The startup later joined the DMZ incubator at [Toronto Metropolitan University](https://www.torontomu.ca/news-events/news/2021/11/startup-helps-children-learn-arabic/).

**Best for:** Babies, toddlers and preschoolers learning first words: colours, animals, greetings, body parts and the alphabet.

**Arabic and ages:** Modern Standard Arabic. Aimed at the early years, roughly 0–5.

**Our take:** If you only choose one channel, this is a strong start. The songs are short, clear and built around vocabulary, so they pair perfectly with our list of [100 Arabic words for kids](/blog/arabic-vocabulary-for-kids). If you prefer vocal-only songs, preview each video first, since many children's channels use instruments.

### 2. Iftah Ya Simsim (افتح يا سمسم)

**What it is:** The Arabic version of Sesame Street. The original ran from 1979 until 1989, and [it relaunched in 2015](https://www.npr.org/2015/09/06/437821382/after-25-year-hiatus-first-arabic-language-sesame-street-opens-again) after a 25-year break, with new original songs and a focus on early learning, including the Arabic letters.

**Best for:** Preschoolers who enjoy puppets, letters and number songs.

**Arabic and ages:** Formal, standard Arabic rather than a regional dialect. Best for about ages 2–6.

**Our take:** Familiar characters make it easy for children who already know Sesame Street to switch into Arabic. Clips and songs are posted on the show's official YouTube channel; look for the verified badge.

### 3. Ahlan Simsim (أهلاً سمسم)

**What it is:** A newer Sesame Workshop series made for children in the Middle East and North Africa, following friends Basma and Jad. [Sesame Workshop](https://sesameworkshop.org/about-us/press-room/ahlan-simsim-friends-are-back-new-season-exciting-adventures-and-playful/) describes it as focused on playful learning and social-emotional skills.

**Best for:** Children aged about 3–8 learning words for feelings, friendship and everyday play.

**Arabic and ages:** Produced in Amman, Jordan, for children across the region, in everyday spoken Arabic that sounds closer to how families in Jordan, Syria and Lebanon talk than to textbook fusha. Sesame Workshop also notes Word of the Day and Number of the Day segments.

**Our take:** A good choice for Levantine families, or for children who need help naming emotions. Pair it with a fusha channel if reading is your main goal.

### 4. Marah KG (مرح كي جي)

**What it is:** A preschool YouTube channel from the Arab production company Pixitoon, built around learning through singing and play. In 2021, [Intaj reported](https://www.intaj.net/media-center/newsroom/marah-kg-channel-among-top-100-channels-worldwide) that it had been ranked among the top 100 children's channels worldwide.

**Best for:** Ages 2–6 who like bright animation and catchy learning songs.

**Arabic and ages:** Arabic; check individual videos for whether they use fusha or dialect.

**Our take:** Plenty of content, which is both a strength and a reason to choose specific songs rather than letting autoplay run. Build a short playlist of the ones you like.

### 5. Omar & Hana Arabic (عمر وهنا)

**What it is:** The Arabic-language channel of the popular Islamic cartoon Omar & Hana, made by the Malaysian studio Digital Durian; the series also streams on the Durioo+ app. Its Arabic YouTube channel launched in July 2020 and features anashid and stories about Islamic values.

**Best for:** Families who want songs about Allah, the Prophet ﷺ, manners and daily duas, in Arabic.

**Arabic and ages:** Arabic. Best for about ages 2–8.

**Our take:** A good bridge between Arabic and Islamic learning. Some songs include instruments; if that matters to your family, see the note below and our guide to the [best Islamic cartoons for kids](/blog/best-islamic-cartoons-for-kids), which covers the Durioo+ vocal-only filter.

### 6. "Mama Zamanha Gaya" (ماما زمانها جاية)

**What it is:** One of the most famous Arabic children's songs ever recorded, sung and composed by the Egyptian artist Mohamed Fawzi. A child waits for Mama to come home with a bag of treats, including a duck that goes "quack quack".

**Best for:** Toddlers, and parents or grandparents who grew up with it.

**Arabic and ages:** Egyptian dialect. Ages 1–5.

**Our take:** This is the song grandparents across the Arab world will join in on. It is less useful for fusha vocabulary, but wonderful for family connection.

### 7. "Dhahab al-Layl" (ذهب الليل)

**What it is:** Another classic children's song by Mohamed Fawzi, often known by its opening line "ذهب الليل، طلع الفجر" (the night has gone, the dawn has risen). In it, a little bird wakes at dawn and meets a cat, and children love acting out the conversation.

**Best for:** Morning routines and playful role-play.

**Arabic and ages:** Mostly Egyptian dialect, with some standard words. Ages 2–6.

**Our take:** A gentle way to start the day, and a chance to teach فَجْر (fajr, dawn), the same word as the Fajr prayer.

### 8. "Ṭalaʿa al-Badru ʿAlaynā" (طلع البدر علينا)

**What it is:** One of the best-known traditional Arabic nasheeds, sung in Muslim communities around the world and widely associated with the welcome the Prophet ﷺ received in Madinah.

**Best for:** All ages, especially for families who prefer vocal-only songs.

**Arabic and ages:** Classical Arabic. All ages.

**Our take:** Many recordings are completely vocal, and the melody is simple enough to sing at home. Explain the words as you sing: الْبَدْر (al-badr) is the full moon.

### 9. "Yalla Tnam Rima" (يلّا تنام ريما)

**What it is:** A traditional Levantine lullaby in which a mother sings her little girl, Rima, to sleep. The Lebanese singer Fairuz recorded a well-known version, and families often swap in their own child's name.

**Best for:** Bedtime with babies and toddlers.

**Arabic and ages:** Levantine dialect. Ages 0–4.

**Our take:** Lullabies are some of the first Arabic children ever hear. You don't need a recording at all: sing it softly yourself.

### 10. Songs you sing yourself

**What it is:** Arabic words set to tunes your child already knows. "Head, shoulders, knees and toes" works beautifully as رَأْس، أَكْتَاف، رُكَب، أَقْدَام (raʾs, aktāf, rukab, aqdām). "Twinkle twinkle" can become a song about نَجْم (najm, star) and قَمَر (qamar, moon).

**Best for:** Every family, including those who prefer no instruments or less screen time.

**Arabic and ages:** Whatever you choose. All ages.

**Our take:** Your voice is the best Arabic song your child will hear. Children don't mind imperfect pronunciation from a parent; they mind silence.

## A note on music and nasheeds

Muslim families make different choices about songs. Scholars hold a range of views on musical instruments, and some families prefer only vocal nasheeds or recitation for young children. Others consider wholesome children's songs a normal part of growing up.

This list doesn't settle that question for you. If you prefer to avoid instruments, choose vocal-only nasheeds, sing songs yourself, and lean on Quran recitation and duas, which children learn in exactly the same way: through rhythm and repetition. Our list of [short surahs for kids](/blog/short-surahs-for-kids) is a natural next step. For guidance specific to your family, ask a scholar or imam you trust.

## How to use Arabic songs to teach Arabic

### Step 1: Choose two or three songs, not twenty

Children learn through repetition. Play the same few songs for a week or two before adding new ones.

### Step 2: Learn the key words first

Pick three to five words from the song, such as the animals in "Dhahab al-Layl", and teach them with pictures or toys. Then the song becomes a chance to hear words your child already knows.

### Step 3: Add actions

Point to your head, flap like a bird, count on your fingers. Movement helps young children remember words and keeps them engaged.

### Step 4: Turn off the screen and sing

After a few listens, sing without the video, in the car, at bath time or while tidying up. The goal is for the song to live in your home, not only on a device.

### Step 5: Use the words outside the song

When you see a rabbit at the park, say "أَرْنَب!" and hum the tune. This is the moment a song word becomes a real word.

## Screen-time tips for Arabic songs

- **Preview first.** Watch a few videos before sharing a channel, and turn off autoplay.
- **Keep it short.** The Canadian Paediatric Society's [screen time guidance](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/behavior-and-development/screen-time-and-young-children) recommends no screen time under age 2, apart from video chatting, and an hour or less a day of routine screen time for ages 2 to 5.
- **Audio counts.** Many of these songs work just as well as audio, with no screen at all.
- **Watch together.** Singing along and talking about the song makes the time far more valuable.
