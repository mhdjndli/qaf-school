Islamic homeschooling in Ontario is fully legal, and it is simpler to start than many parents expect. Ontario does not require you to be a certified teacher, use the provincial curriculum or get anyone's approval; ministry policy asks you to notify your school board in writing. The real questions are which curriculum to use, how to build community for your child, and whether full homeschooling, an Islamic school or a hybrid suits your family best.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Homeschooling is legal in Ontario under the Education Act, and the ministry's **Policy/Program Memorandum 131** sets out how boards treat home-educating families.
> - Ministry policy asks parents to **notify the school board in writing**, with renewal before September 1 each year. No approval or testing is required.
> - You are **not required to follow the Ontario curriculum**, but you should have a plan for literacy, numeracy and assessing progress.
> - Islamic homeschoolers typically combine a secular core (reading, math, science) with an Islamic studies series, Quran with a qualified teacher, and Arabic.
> - Hybrid models, such as homeschooling plus a weekend Islamic program, give many families the best of both worlds.

## Is homeschooling legal in Ontario?

Yes. Under the Education Act, children of compulsory school age must attend school unless they are "receiving satisfactory instruction at home or elsewhere," which is the legal basis for homeschooling. The Ministry of Education's [Policy/Program Memorandum 131 on home schooling](https://www.ontario.ca/document/education-ontario-policy-and-program-direction/policyprogram-memorandum-131) explains how school boards should respond when parents choose to educate at home.

Compulsory attendance in Ontario begins in September of the year a child turns six. That means JK and SK are optional, and families with four- and five-year-olds can homeschool, use a part-time program or enrol in kindergarten without any notification requirement.

### What PPM 131 says in plain language

- **Notify the board in writing.** Parents should send a letter of intent to the school board. The notice includes each child's name, gender and date of birth, your address and phone number, and your signature.
- **Renew each year.** For later years, written notice should be given before September 1.
- **No approval needed.** Once notified, the board should consider the child excused from attendance and send an acknowledgment letter.
- **No investigation by default.** A board should normally not investigate. It may do so only if it has reasonable grounds to doubt that a child is receiving satisfactory instruction.
- **No curriculum mandate.** The memo acknowledges that parents may not follow the Ontario curriculum. Satisfactory instruction centres on having a plan for your child's education, including literacy and numeracy, and a way to assess progress.
- **Optional testing.** Homeschooled children may write EQAO assessments if parents request it from the board.

The [Ontario Federation of Teaching Parents](https://ontariohomeschool.org/legal/letter-of-intent/) (OFTP), a long-standing homeschool association, points out that the Education Act itself does not contain a notification requirement; notification comes from ministry policy. Most families still send the letter because it formally records the child as excused from attendance and avoids truancy questions.

> **Tip:** Keep your letter short and stick to what PPM 131 asks for. The OFTP provides a template and notes that the longer information forms some boards send out are designed for investigations, not routine notification.

## How to start homeschooling in Ontario: step by step

**Step 1: Decide your timing.** You can begin at any point in the year. If your child is already enrolled, you will withdraw them; if they have never attended school, you will notify the board when they reach compulsory school age.

**Step 2: Send your letter of intent.** Address it to the board where your child last attended (or your local board if they have never enrolled). Keep a copy and note the date you sent it.

**Step 3: Write a simple education plan.** One page is enough: subjects, main resources, weekly rhythm and how you will track progress. This is the core of "satisfactory instruction."

**Step 4: Choose your curriculum.** Start with literacy, math and Quran. Add science, social studies, Islamic studies and Arabic as you find your rhythm.

**Step 5: Build community.** Join a homeschool support group or co-op and plan regular time with other Muslim children.

**Step 6: Keep a portfolio.** Save work samples, reading lists and photos of projects. It helps you see growth and is invaluable if a question ever arises.

## Islamic homeschool curriculum options

There is no single "Islamic homeschool curriculum." Most families build one from four layers.

### 1. Secular core: literacy, math and science

Ontario's curriculum documents are freely available on the [Ontario curriculum and resources website](https://www.dcp.edu.gov.on.ca/en/curriculum), and many homeschoolers use them as a checklist of grade-level expectations, especially if they may return to school later. Families then choose phonics, math and science programs that match their child's learning style. Your local public library is one of the most valuable free resources you have.

### 2. Islamic studies series

Several English-language Islamic studies series, designed for weekend or full-time Islamic schools, adapt well to homeschooling. Commonly used options include:

- **IQRA' International Educational Foundation**: a long-established, US-based publisher with a sequenced [Islamic studies curriculum](https://iqrafoundation.com/curriculum/) covering aqidah, fiqh, akhlaq, Seerah and stories of the prophets.
- **Weekend Learning Islamic Studies**: a graded series by Mansur Ahmad and Husain A. Nuri, with textbooks and workbooks starting from early elementary.
- **I Love Islam**: a series from the Islamic Services Foundation with student textbooks, workbooks and teacher/parent guides.
- **Safar Learn about Islam**: a UK-published series that sits within the wider Safar curriculum alongside its Quran-reading and Arabic strands.

Look at sample pages before buying. Check the reading level, how much parent preparation each lesson needs, and whether the content reflects mainstream Sunni teaching your family is comfortable with.

### 3. Quran and tajweed

Quran is the one subject most homeschooling parents choose not to teach entirely alone. Many start a young child on a foundational reader such as the Noorani Qaida (see our comparison of [Noorani Qaida vs Qaida Baghdadia](/blog/noorani-qaida-vs-qaida-baghdadia)), then move to a qualified teacher for tajweed and memorization. [Quran.com](https://quran.com/) offers free recitation audio and translations for review at home. Our guides to [Quran classes for kids in Ottawa](/blog/quran-classes-for-kids-ottawa) and [online vs in-person Quran classes](/blog/online-vs-in-person-quran-classes) can help you choose.

### 4. Arabic

Arabic is easiest for children when it is heard and used daily, not just studied. Families often combine an Arabic textbook series with songs, picture books and conversation at home. Our post on [how to teach Arabic at home](/blog/teach-arabic-at-home) walks through a practical routine.

## Homeschool co-ops and community

A homeschool co-op is a group of families who meet regularly so children learn together, often with parents taking turns teaching subjects they know well. For Muslim families, a co-op can include a shared Quran circle, a science lab day, Arabic games and outdoor time.

Ways to find or build community:

- **Homeschool support groups.** The OFTP maintains a list of [homeschool support groups across Ontario](https://ontariohomeschool.org/resources/support-groups-ontario/).
- **Your masjid.** Many masjids host weekday halaqat, youth programs or classes that homeschoolers can join.
- **Start your own.** Three or four families meeting one morning a week is enough. Agree on a simple rotation and shared expectations about screens, food and adab.
- **Weekend programs.** Structured weekend Islamic programs give homeschooled children a regular peer group and teachers outside the family.

Socialization is the concern parents raise most often. In practice, homeschooled children who have regular co-op days, sports, masjid programs and family visits often have a wide circle; what matters is planning it rather than assuming it will happen.

## Pros and cons of Islamic homeschooling in Ontario

### Advantages

- **Faith woven through the whole day.** Salah times, duas and Islamic manners shape the rhythm of home naturally.
- **Personalized pace.** Children can race ahead in reading or take extra time in math.
- **Flexible schedule.** Travel, family visits and Ramadan routines are easier to accommodate.
- **Lower direct cost.** There is no tuition, though curriculum, classes and activities add up.
- **Family closeness.** Siblings learn together, and parents know exactly what their children are learning.

### Challenges

- **Time and income.** One parent usually reduces paid work. Ontario provides [no funding for homeschoolers](https://ontariohomeschool.org/homeschool-faq/).
- **Parent burnout.** Teaching, cooking, cleaning and parenting all day is demanding, especially with several young children.
- **Specialist subjects.** Quran with tajweed, Arabic and higher-level math or science often need outside teachers.
- **Community takes effort.** Peer time does not happen automatically.
- **High school credentials.** The OFTP notes that home-issued diplomas are not recognized as Ontario Secondary School Diplomas; teens who want an OSSD complete credits through an accredited school.

## Homeschool vs Islamic school vs public school plus weekend program

Here is a side-by-side comparison of the three paths Muslim families in Ontario most often weigh.

| | Islamic homeschooling | Full-time Islamic school | Public school + weekend Islamic program |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cost** | No tuition; curriculum, classes and activities; often one reduced income | Tuition (varies by school) | Public school is free; weekend program fees |
| **Islamic content** | As much as you choose, all day | Daily Quran, Islamic studies, often Arabic | Weekly, typically a few hours |
| **Curriculum** | Your choice; no mandate | Varies; many align with Ontario curriculum | Ontario curriculum |
| **Peer community** | Planned through co-ops, masjid, sports | Built in, Muslim peers | Diverse classmates plus weekend Muslim peers |
| **Parent time** | Very high | Low to moderate | Low to moderate |
| **Flexibility** | Highest | School calendar | School calendar plus weekend schedule |
| **Teachers** | Parents, plus tutors and classes | Trained teachers in one setting | Certified teachers plus weekend teachers |
| **Best for** | Families with time, structure and a strong support network | Families wanting faith and academics integrated daily | Families wanting public school plus regular Islamic learning |

For a deeper look at the two school options, see our comparison of [Islamic school vs public school in Ontario](/blog/islamic-school-vs-public-school-ontario).

## Hybrid options: the middle path

Many families do not choose just one path. Common hybrids include:

1. **Islamic kindergarten, then homeschool.** Children get a strong start in Quran, Arabic and classroom routines in JK and SK, then families homeschool from Grade 1.
2. **Homeschool plus a weekend Islamic program.** Parents handle academics during the week; a weekend program provides Quran, Arabic and Islamic studies with peers and teachers.
3. **Homeschool plus a Quran halaqa.** Families teach academics and Islamic studies at home and send children to a hifz or Quran class several times a week.
4. **Public school plus weekend program.** Children attend public school and build Islamic knowledge on weekends, a widely used model in Ottawa. Our guide to [weekend Islamic schools in Ottawa](/blog/weekend-islamic-schools-ottawa) compares options.

## Is Islamic homeschooling right for your family?

Ask yourselves honestly:

- Can one parent commit several focused hours a day, most days, for years?
- Do you have a support network: a co-op, family help, other homeschooling parents?
- Who will teach Quran with tajweed and Arabic if you cannot?
- How will your child spend regular time with other Muslim children?
- What is your plan if homeschooling stops working for a child or for you?

If the answers feel solid, homeschooling can be a deeply rewarding way to raise children with faith at the centre. If several feel shaky, a hybrid or an Islamic school may serve your child better, and there is no shame in that. Many families move between options as children grow.

## The bottom line

Ontario gives families wide freedom to homeschool: notify your board, plan for literacy and numeracy, and you are free to build an education around your faith. The families who thrive are those who plan for the hard parts, especially Quran instruction, Arabic and community, from the start. Whatever path you choose, consistency matters more than perfection.
