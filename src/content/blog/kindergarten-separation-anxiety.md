Kindergarten separation anxiety is the crying, clinging and worry many 4- and 5-year-olds feel when a parent leaves them at school. It is a normal stage of development, and for many children it fades within a few weeks when adults follow a calm, consistent plan. This guide gives you that plan: why drop-off tears happen, how to tell normal worry from a bigger problem, a four-week drop-off routine, word-for-word scripts, and comforting duas for Muslim families.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Some separation anxiety at the start of JK or SK is normal, especially in the first weeks and after holidays or illness.
> - A short, warm, predictable goodbye works better than lingering, sneaking out or coming back.
> - Staying home after tears usually makes the next drop-off harder. Gentle but firm encouragement helps children learn they can cope.
> - Praise brave behaviour, however small, and work as a team with the teacher.
> - Talk to your child's health care provider if worry interferes with daily life, keeps going after the routine is familiar, or comes with frequent stomach aches or headaches.

## What is kindergarten separation anxiety?

Separation anxiety is a child's distress when they are apart from a parent or main caregiver. In kindergarten, it usually shows up at drop-off as crying, clinging, refusing to go in, or "I don't want you to go."

It happens because young children are still learning that the people they love will come back. The American Academy of Pediatrics notes that separation anxiety often begins in infancy and continues through the toddler and preschool years, and that it is [rare for it to persist daily after the preschool years](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/ages-stages/toddler/Pages/Soothing-Your-Childs-Separation-Anxiety.aspx). Starting JK or SK, a new building, a new routine and a full day away from home can bring it back.

### When it tends to appear

- **The first days or weeks** of JK or SK.
- **Day two or three,** once your child realizes you really will leave every day.
- **After weekends, holidays or illness,** when routines have been broken.
- **During changes at home,** such as a new baby, a move or a parent travelling.
- **When a child is tired or hungry,** which makes every feeling bigger.

Separation anxiety does not mean your child is unhappy at school or that you have done something wrong. Many children who cry hard at the door are playing happily a few minutes later.

## Normal separation anxiety vs something more

The Canadian Paediatric Society describes brief upset when separating from a parent as typical for preschool-age children. It lists signs that may point to an [anxiety disorder](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/mentalhealth/anxiety-and-anxiety-disorders), such as constant clinging or inconsolable crying even after a routine is familiar.

| Usually normal | Worth discussing with a doctor |
|---|---|
| Tears or clinging at drop-off that ease soon after you leave | Inconsolable crying that continues long after the routine is familiar |
| Worse after weekends, holidays or illness | Getting worse over weeks rather than better |
| Happy to tell you about school later | Constant worry that a parent will be hurt or not come back |
| Occasional "I don't want to go" | Frequent stomach aches or headaches when separation is expected |
| Settles within the first weeks | Fear that interferes with sleep, play or daily activities |

If your child fits the left column, a consistent plan is usually all that is needed. If several items on the right fit, keep using the plan and also talk to your child's doctor.

## The four-week drop-off plan

This plan works best when everyone who does drop-off follows the same steps. Write it down and share it with your partner and the teacher.

| Week | Focus | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Before school starts | Practice | Short separations with trusted adults, school visits, books about kindergarten |
| Week 1 | Predictability | Same arrival time, same goodbye ritual, same pick-up person and time |
| Week 2 | Confidence | Shorten your goodbye. Praise every brave moment at pick-up |
| Week 3 | Independence | Your child carries their own bag and walks the last few steps to the teacher |
| Week 4 | Review | Check in with the teacher. Keep the routine; adjust only one thing at a time |

### Step 1: Practise separations before school starts

The Ontario government suggests [planning short, safe sessions](https://www.ontario.ca/page/preparing-kindergarten) where you are not with your child, such as a few hours with a grandparent or a trusted friend. Start short, then make them longer. Each time, come back exactly when you said you would.

### Step 2: Create a goodbye ritual

Choose a goodbye your child can predict: two hugs, a kiss on the hand, "Assalamu alaykum, see you after outside time." The AAP recommends keeping goodbyes short and doing the same ritual every day.

### Step 3: Prepare the night before

The Canadian Paediatric Society suggests preparing the night before so mornings are not rushed. Pack the bag together, lay out clothes and talk through tomorrow in three simple steps.

### Step 4: Hand over, then leave

Walk your child to the teacher, say your ritual, and leave confidently, even if your child is crying. A teacher can comfort and redirect a child much more easily once the parent has gone.

### Step 5: Return exactly when you promised

Describe pick-up in terms your child understands: "after lunch and outside play," not "at 3 o'clock." Then be there. Each reliable pick-up teaches your child that goodbyes end with you coming back.

### Step 6: Notice and praise bravery

At pick-up, name what your child did: "You walked in with your teacher even though you felt sad. That was brave." The CPS encourages parents of young children to notice brave behaviours, no matter how small.

## Drop-off scripts and mistakes to avoid

What you say in the 30 seconds at the door matters. Keep it short, calm and certain.

### Scripts that help

- **Naming the feeling:** "You feel sad that I'm leaving. That's okay. Your teacher will help you."
- **Showing confidence:** "I know you can do this. I'll see you after outside play."
- **Giving a job:** "Can you show your teacher where your water bottle goes?"
- **Reminding of the routine:** "First the work time, then snack, then lunch, then I come."

### Mistakes that make drop-off harder

**Sneaking out.** It can feel kinder, but it often makes children more anxious about when you will disappear next. Always say goodbye.

**Lingering or coming back.** Several goodbyes, or returning for "one more hug," tells your child the door is still open to negotiation. The AAP warns that prolonging the goodbye prolongs the anxiety.

**Bargaining with big rewards.** A small sticker for walking in is fine. A toy for "not crying" puts pressure on feelings your child cannot control.

**Keeping your child home after tears.** The CPS advises preventing avoidance with gentle but firm encouragement. Staying home brings quick relief but teaches that school is something to escape.

**Showing your own worry.** Children read your face. If you feel anxious, take a breath before you reach the door, smile, and save your tears for the car.

> **Tip:** Ask the teacher to send a quick update if your child settles after you leave. At QAF, teachers can share a message or photo through ClassDojo, which reassures many parents in the first weeks.

## Tools that help at home

**A comfort object.** Both the Ontario government and the CPS suggest letting your child bring a favourite toy or small item from home, if the school allows it. A family photo in the backpack or a small heart drawn on both your hands can work too.

**Books and role-play.** Read picture books about starting school, then play "school" with toys. Let your child be the teacher and you be the nervous child.

**A visual schedule.** Draw the school day in four pictures: arrive, play, lunch, home. Point to them in the morning.

**Calm-body skills.** Practise "smell the flower, blow out the candle" breathing at home when your child is calm, so it is ready when they are upset. The CPS recommends teaching and modelling coping skills like deep breathing.

**Enough sleep and food.** A tired, hungry child has less capacity to cope. Protect bedtime and offer breakfast before school.

**Special time after school.** Ten minutes of your full attention after pick-up, with no phones, refills your child's tank.

## Faith-based comfort for Muslim families

Islam gives children and parents words for worried moments. Use them gently, as comfort, never as pressure or as a reason a child "shouldn't" feel scared.

**Leaving home together.** Say the leaving-home dua at the door each morning (Sunan Abi Dawud 5095):

بِسْمِ اللَّهِ تَوَكَّلْتُ عَلَى اللَّهِ لاَ حَوْلَ وَلاَ قُوَّةَ إِلاَّ بِاللَّهِ

*Bismillah, tawakkaltu 'alallah, la hawla wa la quwwata illa billah.* "In the name of Allah, I place my trust in Allah; there is no might and no power except with Allah."

**A dua for a nervous heart.** Prophet Musa (peace be upon him) asked Allah to ease his task (Surah Ta-Ha 20:25–26). Children can learn it in a few days:

رَبِّ ٱشْرَحْ لِى صَدْرِى وَيَسِّرْ لِىٓ أَمْرِى

*Rabbish-rah li sadri, wa yassir li amri.* "My Lord, expand my chest for me, and ease my task for me."

**A dua for parents.** While you walk back to the car, make dua for your child, for example from Surah Al-Furqan 25:74:

رَبَّنَا هَبْ لَنَا مِنْ أَزْوَٰجِنَا وَذُرِّيَّـٰتِنَا قُرَّةَ أَعْيُنٍ

*Rabbana hab lana min azwajina wa dhurriyyatina qurrata a'yun.* "Our Lord, grant us from our spouses and offspring comfort to our eyes."

You can explain tawakkul simply: "We do our part, we say bismillah, and we trust Allah to take care of us." ## Working with your child's teacher

Teachers have helped many children through drop-off tears, so make them your partner.

- **Agree on a handover.** Decide together where you will say goodbye and who will receive your child.
- **Share what comforts your child.** A favourite activity, a calming word in your home language, or a job they enjoy.
- **Ask how your child does after you leave.** Many parents are surprised by how quickly their child settles.
- **Keep the teacher informed** about changes at home, such as a new sibling or a parent away.
- **Review after two to four weeks.** Ask what the teacher sees and adjust one thing at a time.

In a classroom with a predictable rhythm, children often settle faster because they know what comes next. ## When to get extra help

Talk to your child's doctor or another health care provider if, as the CPS describes, fears start to interfere with normal daily activities, your child often seems very anxious, or they often have physical symptoms like headaches or stomach aches. Also reach out if drop-off is getting worse over several weeks rather than better.

In Ottawa, [1Call1Click.ca](https://www.1call1click.ca/) is a provincially funded service that helps children, youth and families get mental health and neurodevelopmental care. Asking for help early is a sign of good parenting, not failure.

## The bottom line

Kindergarten separation anxiety is normal, usually temporary, and very manageable. Practise short separations, use the same short goodbye every day, leave confidently, return exactly when you promised, and praise every brave step. Work with the teacher, use duas as comfort, and get extra help if worry starts to take over daily life.
