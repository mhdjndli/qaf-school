French Immersion vs English is one of the first big decisions Ottawa parents face, usually right after kindergarten. In French Immersion, a large share of the school day, often more than half, is taught in French. In the English program, almost everything is in English, with a daily or weekly French class. For Muslim and Arabic-speaking families, there is a third language in the picture, which changes the question from "Is French Immersion worth it?" to "How do we keep Arabic, English and French all growing?" This guide answers both.

> **Key takeaways**
> - In Ottawa's English boards, every kindergarten child already gets 50% French. The real choice comes in Grade 1 (OCDSB) or Grade 4 (OCSB).
> - OCDSB Early French Immersion is 60% French in Grades 1 to 6, with English and math taught in English. The English program has four one-hour Core French blocks a week.
> - OCSB gives every child 25% French in Grades 1 to 3, then families choose Core French or 50% French Immersion in Grade 4.
> - You do not need to speak French at home. Canadian research shows children with other home languages can do well in immersion.
> - For trilingual families, the biggest risk is usually losing the home language, not falling behind in French or English.

## French Immersion vs English at a glance

Here is how the main Ottawa pathways compare for elementary school. Figures come from the [OCDSB](https://www.ocdsb.ca/programs-learning/elementary/early-french-immersion) and the [OCSB French programs page](https://www.ocsb.ca/french/).

| | OCDSB English with Core French | OCDSB Early French Immersion | OCSB Core French | OCSB French Immersion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Kindergarten** | 50% French / 50% English | 50% French / 50% English | 50% French / 50% English | 50% French / 50% English |
| **Grades 1–3** | English, plus four 1-hour French blocks a week | 60% French (entry in Gr. 1, 2 or 3) | Extended French, 25% French | Extended French, 25% French |
| **Grades 4–6** | English plus Core French | 60% French | About 15% French | 50% French |
| **Grades 7–8** | English plus Core French | 50% French | 1 of 8 classes in French | 4 of 8 classes in French |
| **Taught in English** | Everything except French class | English language arts and math | Everything except French | Half of subjects |
| **Available where** | Every OCDSB school | Designated EFI schools | All OCSB schools | All OCSB schools |

The OCDSB is also phasing out Middle French Immersion (a Grade 4 entry), with the last intake in September 2028. French-language boards (CEPEO and CECCE) are different again: they are not immersion but full French-language schools for eligible families. See [Ottawa school boards compared](/blog/ottawa-school-boards-compared) for who qualifies.

## What French Immersion actually means

French Immersion is a program for children who are not francophone, in which French is the language of instruction for a large part of the day. Children learn French by learning science, social studies, the arts and other subjects in it, rather than by studying French as a subject alone.

In the OCDSB's Early French Immersion program, about 60% of instruction in Grades 1 to 6 is in French. The board notes that English language arts and math are taught in English, and generally all other subjects are taught in French. The curriculum expectations are the same as in the English program.

The **English program** in the OCDSB, called English with Core French, teaches every subject in English, with four one-hour Core French blocks each week. Children still learn French, but more slowly and mainly as a subject.

### How the OCSB does it differently

The Ottawa Catholic School Board gives every student the same start: 50/50 kindergarten, then **Extended French** at 25% in Grades 1 to 3. In Grade 4, families choose Core French (about 15%) or French Immersion (50%). The OCSB says families who want high school French Immersion need to choose Immersion in Grade 4, and teachers raise this decision in Grade 3.

## Key entry points and deadlines in Ottawa

Timing matters more than many parents realize.

- **OCDSB:** From September 2026, children can enter Early French Immersion at the start of Grade 1, 2 or 3 at their designated school. Students with previous immersion experience can join later. Current OCDSB families receive program information from their school in January, with a deadline in early February (it was February 6 for the 2026–27 year).
- **OCSB:** All students follow the same path until Grade 4, when you choose Core or Immersion.
- **New to Ottawa:** The OCDSB says students coming from French Immersion in another board can continue their French studies. Ask the OCSB about your child's situation if you are moving there.

If you are starting at an Islamic or private kindergarten, register with the board in the winter before Grade 1 and state your pathway choice then. ## Pros and cons of French Immersion

### Advantages

- **Stronger French.** Children reach a much higher level of French than in a Core program, which is valuable in a bilingual city and country.
- **Early start.** Young children pick up pronunciation and listening skills easily.
- **Ottawa opportunities.** French opens doors in a city where many jobs, especially in the federal public service, value bilingualism.
- **Same curriculum.** Immersion students cover the same Ontario expectations as English-program students.

### Disadvantages

- **Location.** In the OCDSB, immersion may be offered at a different school from your English-program school, which can affect friendships, siblings and busing.
- **Homework support.** If no one at home reads French, helping with homework can be harder.
- **Less English instruction time.** English reading may develop on a slightly different timeline in early grades, although math and English are taught in English in the OCDSB program.
- **Fatigue for some children.** Learning content in a new language takes effort, especially for children already managing two languages.

## Pros and cons of the English program

### Advantages

- **Simplicity.** Homework is in a language parents usually know, which matters for families still building their own English.
- **Closer to home.** Every OCDSB and OCSB school offers it.
- **Room for Arabic.** With less pressure from a third academic language, some families find it easier to keep Arabic strong at home.

### Disadvantages

- **Weaker French.** Core French alone rarely produces fluent speakers by Grade 8.
- **Fewer French options later.** Moving into immersion later is possible in some grades but becomes harder as the gap grows.

## Does your child need French at home?

No. French Immersion was designed for children whose families do not speak French. The OCSB, for example, says children do not need French at home for its bilingual kindergarten.

Children whose home language is neither English nor French, known in Canadian research as **allophone** students, are sometimes discouraged from immersion. But a 2019 study in the [Canadian Journal of Applied Linguistics](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/CJAL/article/view/28620) notes that earlier research has documented strong motivation, family support and academic achievement among allophone students learning French in Canada, while also finding they are sometimes excluded because of limited English. The OCDSB's own EFI page states that academic ability is not related to performance in French language skills.

In other words, Arabic at home is not a reason to avoid immersion. It is a reason to plan carefully.

## Three languages: what Muslim and Arabic-speaking families should consider

Many families we meet are juggling Arabic (or Urdu, Somali, Turkish, Farsi and others) at home, English in the community, and French at school, plus Quranic Arabic for worship. That is a lot, and it is also very achievable. Children around the world grow up with three or more languages.

### Protect the home language first

The Canadian Paediatric Society's guidance for newcomer families, [Caring for Kids New to Canada](https://kidsnewtocanada.ca/screening/language-acquisition), is clear: a strong first language helps children learn a second, bilingualism does not cause language delay, and parents should speak, read, sing and play in their home language. It also notes that losing the home language can strain parent-child communication and cultural identity.

For Muslim families, Arabic carries extra weight because it is the language of the Quran and salah. The risk in a trilingual household is rarely that a child "can't cope". It is that Arabic quietly fades as English and French take over the school day. We cover practical strategies in [raising trilingual kids in Canada](/blog/raising-trilingual-kids-canada) and [why early Arabic immersion matters](/blog/why-early-arabic-immersion-matters).

### Questions to ask yourselves

- **Who speaks what at home?** If both parents speak Arabic, keep Arabic the home language. School will take care of English and French.
- **Can we support French homework?** If not, ask the school about homework help, French libraries, and tutoring, and plan for it.
- **How much time does our child have?** Weekend Quran school, Arabic classes and sports all compete with homework. Be realistic.
- **What does our child enjoy?** A child who loves languages and is a confident speaker often thrives in immersion. A child who is struggling with speech or reading may need extra support, and a speech-language assessment in both languages, before you decide.

### A note on Quranic Arabic and French

Some parents worry that French "uses up" the space for Arabic. In the classroom, we find that young children who are used to hearing more than one language are often comfortable with new sounds, including Arabic letters. The key is consistency. Daily Arabic, even 15 minutes of stories and conversation, and steady Quran practice matter more than which school program you choose.

## Decision guide: which program fits your family?

**French Immersion is likely a good fit if:**

- Your child is a confident communicator in at least one language.
- You value strong French for your child's future in Ottawa or Canada.
- You have a plan for keeping Arabic strong at home or through a weekend program.
- The immersion school is reasonably close or bus-eligible.

**The English program is likely a better fit if:**

- Your child is already working hard to catch up in English.
- You want maximum time and energy for Arabic and Quran at home.
- The immersion school is far away or would separate siblings.
- Your family would struggle to support homework in French.

**Consider a French-language school (CEPEO or CECCE) if:**

- A parent's first language is French, or a parent was educated in French in Canada, or the family is eligible through an admission committee.
- French is genuinely used at home. This is common in families from North and West Africa and Lebanon.

**Remember you can change course.** OCDSB children can still enter immersion in Grade 2 or 3, OCSB families choose in Grade 4, and students can move from immersion to the English program later.

## Conclusion

French Immersion vs English is not a test of how ambitious you are as a parent. Both pathways cover the same Ontario curriculum. Immersion gives stronger French, while the English program is simpler and leaves more room for other languages. For Muslim and Arabic-speaking families, the most important decision is not which school program, but how you will keep the home language and Quranic Arabic alive alongside it.
