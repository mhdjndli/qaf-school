A Quran competition in Canada is usually a one-day or weekend event, hosted by a mosque, Islamic centre or charity, where children and youth recite memorized portions to a panel of judges in categories set by surah, juz or age. Most Canadian competitions run in winter or around Ramadan, and registration often closes weeks before. With a steady three-month plan, almost any child who is memorizing regularly can take part.

This Quran competition Canada guide lists the events we could verify, explains how judging works, and gives you a week-by-week plan to prepare your child calmly and with the right intention.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Canadian Quran competitions include mosque and charity events in Ottawa and Gatineau, ISNA Canada’s annual competition in Mississauga, and MIST for high-school students.
> - Most competitions group children by age or by amount memorized (a few surahs up to the full Quran).
> - Judges typically assess memorization accuracy, tajweed and the quality of recitation.
> - Start preparing about 10–12 weeks before the event, and pick a category your child can recite confidently, not the hardest one.
> - The real prize is a stronger connection with the Quran; keep intentions sincere and celebrate effort.

## How Quran competitions are judged

**Most Quran competitions judge three things: how accurately the child has memorized, how correctly they apply tajweed, and the overall quality of their recitation.** Each organizer weights these differently, so read the rules sheet carefully.

| What judges listen for | What it means | How to practise |
|---|---|---|
| **Memorization (hifz)** | Reciting without skipping, swapping or forgetting words; how many prompts the child needs | Random-start drills, daily revision |
| **Tajweed** | Correct letters and rules: ghunnah, madd, qalqalah, heavy and light letters | Weekly checks with a teacher |
| **Quality of recitation** | Steady pace, clear voice, stopping and starting in sensible places | Recording and listening back |

In many competitions, the judge chooses where the child begins. In ISNA Canada’s 2026 format, for example, judges asked for a passage from the participant’s chosen category, then tested finalists on a different passage. That is why random-start practice matters so much.

The Prophet ﷺ said: “Beautify the Qur’an with your voices” ([Sunan Abi Dawud 1468](https://sunnah.com/abudawud:1468), graded sahih by al-Albani). A beautiful voice is welcome, but in children’s categories, accuracy and tajweed almost always matter more than melody.

## Is my child ready for a Quran competition?

Your child is likely ready if they can recite their chosen portion from memory with few mistakes, can start from a point someone else picks, and can sit calmly in front of an adult they do not know.

### Suggested starting points by age

- **Ages 5–7:** A local mosque or school competition with a few short surahs from Juz Amma. Keep it light and fun.
- **Ages 8–12:** Age-based categories (like the Aylmer format) or a single-juz category. This is a sweet spot for a first “real” competition.
- **Ages 13–15 and up:** Multi-juz categories, or MIST in high school, if they have the revision habit to match.

**Choose the category your child can recite confidently, not the hardest one they have touched.** A child who recites one juz beautifully learns far more than one who stumbles through five.

## How to prepare: a 12-week plan

This plan assumes your child already knows most of the portion. If they still need to memorize it, add time, or choose a smaller category. Our [hifz revision schedule guide](/blog/hifz-revision-schedule) explains how to build daily revision around new lessons.

**Step 1 (Weeks 1–2): Choose the category and read the rules.** Confirm the date, the required portion, the age rules and the registration deadline. Register early, because some events cap spots.

**Step 2 (Weeks 1–4): Fill the gaps.** Recite the full portion to a teacher and mark every weak spot in a notebook or Mushaf with a pencil. Focus new effort on those ayat only.

**Step 3 (Weeks 3–8): Revise daily, in rotations.** Divide the portion into sections and revise one or two every day so the whole portion is covered each week. Keep sessions short for young children.

**Step 4 (Weeks 5–10): Practise random starts.** Have a parent or sibling open the Mushaf anywhere in the portion, read the first few words, and let your child continue. This is the single best competition drill.

**Step 5 (Weeks 6–10): Polish tajweed.** Ask a teacher to listen once a week and correct one or two rules at a time. Our guide to [tajweed rules for kids](/blog/tajweed-rules-for-kids) has simple examples you can review at home.

**Step 6 (Weeks 8–11): Do mock competitions.** Seat your child in front of two or three family members, have one act as judge, and record the recitation. Listen back together, gently.

**Step 7 (Week 12): Light review and rest.** In the final week, revise but do not cram. Sleep, food and calm matter more now than one more round.

### A sample week (ages 8–12)

| Day | Focus (20–30 minutes) |
|---|---|
| Monday | Revise section A + random-start drill |
| Tuesday | Revise section B + tajweed point of the week |
| Wednesday | Revise section C + record and listen |
| Thursday | Revise section D + weak-spot list |
| Friday | Light review of the week’s mistakes |
| Saturday | Mock competition for family |
| Sunday | Teacher check (class or halaqah) |

## Competition day tips

1. **Arrive early.** Most events ask participants to check in on time; ISNA Canada’s 2026 guidance suggested arriving 10–15 minutes before your slot.
2. **Pack water, a snack and wudu-friendly clothes.** Long waits are common.
3. **Warm up quietly.** A few ayat in a soft voice settles nerves.
4. **Teach a “restart” phrase.** If your child gets stuck, they can pause, take a breath and continue from the start of the ayah, as the judge allows.
5. **Stay calm yourself.** Children read their parents’ faces. Smile, make dua and let the judges do their job.

## Keeping the right intention

Healthy competition in good deeds is encouraged in Islam. Allah says of the reward of the righteous in Paradise:

وَفِى ذَٰلِكَ فَلْيَتَنَافَسِ ٱلْمُتَنَـٰفِسُونَ

“So for this let the competitors compete.” ([Surah Al-Mutaffifin 83:26](https://quran.com/83/26), Sahih International)

At the same time, the first hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari reminds us that “the reward of deeds depends upon the intentions” ([Sahih al-Bukhari 1](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:1)). A well-known hadith in Sahih Muslim (1905) also warns against reciting the Quran only so that people will praise you as a reciter.

How to keep intentions sincere with children:

- Before the event, say together: “We are doing this to please Allah and to recite His words well.”
- Praise effort and preparation, not trophies.
- Never compare your child with siblings or classmates.
- Remind them of the reward for the one who finds recitation hard: a double reward ([Sahih al-Bukhari 4937](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:4937)).

## After the competition: wins, losses and next steps

Whatever the result, the weeks of revision were the real achievement. If your child placed, celebrate modestly and thank Allah together. If they did not, ask what they enjoyed, what they would practise differently, and whether they want to try again next year.

Many families find that a competition gives a child a clear goal for the next stretch of memorization. If motivation dips afterwards, our guide on [how to motivate kids to learn Quran](/blog/motivating-kids-to-learn-quran) has age-specific ideas.
