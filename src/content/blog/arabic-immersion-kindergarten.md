An Arabic immersion kindergarten is a JK/SK program where Arabic is used as a working language of the classroom, for routines, songs, stories, instructions and play, rather than taught only as a 30-minute subject. Children learn Arabic the way they learned their first language: by hearing it constantly in meaningful situations and gradually using it themselves. This guide explains how immersion works, what the research says about outcomes, and how to tell whether it suits your child.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Immersion means children learn *through* Arabic, not just *about* Arabic. The amount of Arabic in the day is what separates immersion from a language class.
> - Canada has decades of research on French immersion: children in well-run immersion programs generally keep pace in English. Arabic-specific research is far thinner, so expect similar principles, not guarantees.
> - Most children understand far more than they say at first. A quiet period of weeks or months is normal.
> - Your family does not need to speak Arabic at home for a child to thrive in immersion.
> - Look for warm, fluent teachers, predictable routines, and a program that still covers Ontario's kindergarten expectations.

## What is an Arabic immersion kindergarten?

In a regular kindergarten with an Arabic class, children spend most of the day in English and meet Arabic in a short, separate lesson. In an Arabic immersion kindergarten, Arabic runs through the day itself. The teacher greets children in Arabic, gives instructions in Arabic, sings the tidy-up song in Arabic, reads stories in Arabic and talks with children in Arabic while they build, paint and play.

The key idea is that children acquire a language when they need it to understand and take part in what is happening around them. A four-year-old who wants a turn on the scooter quickly learns دَوْرِي (my turn). A child who hears اِغْسِلْ يَدَيْكَ (wash your hands) before every snack knows exactly what it means within days.

Canadian parents often know this model already from French immersion, where children learn math, stories and science in French from kindergarten onward. Arabic immersion applies the same principle to Arabic, usually in an Islamic school setting that also teaches Quran, duas and Islamic manners.

## Types of immersion at a glance

"Immersion" is used loosely, so it helps to know the main models. The terms below are how language educators commonly describe them.

| Model | How much Arabic | What it looks like | Typical outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total immersion | Nearly all of the day | Arabic for routines, play, stories and early literacy; English added later | Strongest listening and speaking in Arabic |
| Partial immersion | A large share of the day, often half or more | Some subjects or times of day in Arabic, others in English | Solid Arabic with steady English literacy |
| Dual-language (two-way) | Shared, often 50/50 or 80/20 | Classes mix children who speak Arabic at home with those who don't | Both groups build both languages |
| Arabic as a subject | A daily or weekly lesson | Separate Arabic class within an English day | Letter knowledge and vocabulary; limited speaking |

There is no single "right" model. What matters is that the amount of Arabic is high enough, and consistent enough, to make Arabic part of the child's daily life. A program that calls itself immersion but uses English for most instructions is closer to a language class, which is still useful but will produce different results.

## How immersion works in a kindergarten day

Young children are not overwhelmed by a new language when adults make meaning clear. Skilled immersion teachers rely on a few techniques that you can spot during a visit:

1. **Routines in Arabic.** The same phrases at the same moments every day (arrival, circle, snack, tidy-up, dismissal) give children a scaffold of language they understand instantly.
2. **Gestures, pictures and objects.** The teacher points, acts out, shows and models, so children can follow even before they know the words.
3. **Songs and chants.** Repetition through music builds vocabulary and pronunciation without pressure.
4. **Back-and-forth talk.** The teacher responds to what children say and do, in Arabic, and invites them to answer. Researchers at Harvard's Center on the Developing Child call this kind of responsive exchange ["serve and return"](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/science/key-concepts/serve-and-return/), and it is central to how young children build language.
5. **Accepting English answers at first.** A child who answers an Arabic question in English has understood. Good teachers welcome the answer, then model it back in Arabic.
6. **Hands-on learning.** Counting beads, sorting colours and sand-tray letter tracing make Arabic concrete and memorable.

In our classroom we have found that the first few weeks are mostly about comfort: children learn that they are safe, that the routine is predictable, and that they can make themselves understood. Language follows.

## What the research says about immersion outcomes

The largest body of evidence on early immersion comes from Canada's French immersion programs, studied since the 1960s. Summarizing this work, researcher Fred Genesee of McGill University has reported that immersion students generally perform as well as English-program peers in English and academic subjects, while developing strong skills in the second language ([summary from the University of Minnesota's CARLA](https://archive.carla.umn.edu/immersion/acie/vol3/Dec1999_ImmersResearch.html)).

One early study is especially relevant to kindergarten. Holobow, Genesee, Lambert and colleagues followed kindergarten children who spent half their academic day in French and found they did as well in English as matched peers in all-English classrooms, including children from lower-income families ([Applied Psycholinguistics, 1987](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/applied-psycholinguistics/article/abs/effectiveness-of-partial-french-immersion-for-children-from-different-social-class-and-ethnic-backgrounds/BE858AB323D7B3CFA76F84957E18CB3B)).

Two honest caveats:

- **Arabic-specific research is limited.** We could not find large, long-term studies of Arabic immersion kindergartens. The French immersion findings describe general principles of how children learn languages, which very likely apply, but they are not proof for any particular Arabic program.
- **Arabic has its own challenge.** Spoken dialects differ from Modern Standard Arabic (fusha), the Arabic of books and school. Children from Arabic-speaking homes may need to bridge their home dialect and classroom fusha, while children from non-Arabic homes usually learn fusha first. Our guide to [fusha vs dialect](/blog/fusha-vs-dialect-for-kids) explains why this matters.

## Realistic outcomes by the end of kindergarten

Every child is different, and outcomes depend on the program, the child's starting point and how long they attend. With those caveats, these are common milestones for a child who attends a strong Arabic immersion kindergarten for two years (JK and SK):

| Skill | What many children can do by the end of SK |
|---|---|
| Listening | Follow everyday classroom instructions and short stories in Arabic |
| Speaking | Use greetings, polite phrases and short sentences about familiar topics |
| Vocabulary | Name colours, numbers, family members, foods, body parts and classroom objects |
| Early reading | Recognize Arabic letters and sounds, and begin reading short vowelled syllables and words |
| Writing | Trace and form many letters; write their name in Arabic |
| Islamic phrases | Use بِسْمِ اللَّهِ, الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ, إِنْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ and everyday duas naturally |
| English | Meet the same kindergarten expectations as peers in English programs |

Speaking usually lags behind understanding. Many children go through a "silent period" in which they absorb a great deal before saying much. Parents sometimes worry that nothing is happening, then hear their child sing an entire Arabic song at home one evening.

## Will Arabic immersion hurt my child's English?

This is the most common worry, and the Canadian immersion research is reassuring: children in well-designed immersion programs generally keep pace in English. Skills such as phonological awareness (hearing the sounds in words), print awareness and vocabulary-building strategies transfer between languages.

Three things help English stay strong:

- **The program still covers Ontario's expectations.** In Ontario, kindergarten programs follow [The Ontario Curriculum, Kindergarten](https://www.dcp.edu.gov.on.ca/en/curriculum/kindergarten), whose 2026 edition is organized into four strands: Belonging and Contributing, Self-Regulation and Well-Being, Foundations of Language and Mathematics, and Problem Solving and Innovating. Ask any immersion program how it addresses these.
- **You read in English (or your home language) at home.** Daily read-alouds in your strongest language build vocabulary and a love of books that carries over to every language.
- **You talk a lot.** Rich conversation at home, in any language, is one of the best predictors of later literacy.

## Who thrives in immersion, and when to get advice

Most children do well in immersion, including those whose parents do not speak Arabic at all. Children who tend to settle quickly are those who have a consistent routine, attend regularly and hear encouragement at home.

Some situations call for a conversation with the teacher and, where needed, a professional:

- **Suspected speech or language delay.** [HealthLinkBC](https://www.healthlinkbc.ca/healthwise/speech-and-language-delays-common-misconceptions) notes that children growing up with two languages do not have more difficulty learning to talk, read and write, though some may start speaking slightly later. But if your child shows signs of delay in their home language, ask a speech-language pathologist for an assessment rather than waiting.
- **Strong anxiety about not understanding.** Some children need a slower start, more visuals and a buddy. A good teacher will adapt.
- **Very irregular attendance.** Immersion depends on daily exposure. Frequent absences make it harder for a child to keep up.

## What to look for in an Arabic immersion kindergarten

When you visit, notice:

- **How much Arabic you actually hear.** Count how often the teacher speaks Arabic during a 15-minute window.
- **Teacher fluency and warmth.** Clear, natural Arabic and genuine affection for young children matter more than any material.
- **Visual support.** Picture schedules, labels and props that make meaning obvious.
- **Play and movement.** Four-year-olds learn best when they are active, not when they sit through drills.
- **Progress communication.** How will you know what your child is learning?
- **Ontario curriculum alignment.** How literacy, math and self-regulation are covered alongside Arabic.
