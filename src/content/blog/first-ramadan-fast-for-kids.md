A first fast for kids should be short, planned and completely optional: a few hours on a weekend, with a good suhoor, plenty of water before dawn and a parent ready to end it at any sign of trouble. Children do not have to fast in Islam until puberty, so a first fast is practice. Its job is to build love for Ramadan, not to test endurance. This guide covers the right age, a step-by-step plan, Ottawa fasting hours for Ramadan 2027 and the health signs that mean it is time to eat.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Fasting becomes obligatory at puberty. Before that, a child’s first fast is training, and it can be as short as breakfast to lunch.
> - Many families start practice fasts between about 7 and 10, but readiness matters more than age: health, willingness and how your child handles hunger.
> - Build up in steps over weeks or even years: half-day fasts, then longer fasts on weekends, then full days.
> - Stop the fast straight away for dizziness, vomiting, confusion or very little urine. If your child has diabetes or any medical condition, talk to your doctor first.
> - Ramadan 2027 is expected to begin around Monday, February 8 (depending on moon sighting), with Ottawa fasts of roughly 11.5 to 13 hours.

## What age should a child start fasting?

There is no single “right” age for a first fast. Fasting is required only once a child reaches puberty. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said the pen is lifted from three people, including “a boy till he reaches puberty” (Sunan Abi Dawud 4403). Before that, any fast your child keeps is voluntary practice.

Training children gently is still part of the Sunnah tradition. Ar-Rubayyi’ bint Mu’awwidh described how, after the Prophet announced the fast of Ashura, the companions had their young children fast too. They made the children toys of wool to play with until it was time to break the fast ([Sahih al-Bukhari 1960](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:1960)). Notice the spirit of that report: the children were included, comforted and distracted, not pushed.

In practice, many Muslim families try a first half-day fast somewhere between ages 7 and 10, and add longer fasts as children approach puberty. Health guidance from the [Saudi Ministry of Health](https://www.moh.gov.sa/en/awarenessplateform/SeasonalAndFestivalHealth/Educational-Content/Pages/Children-Ramadan.aspx) says the same thing in different words: the child should be willing, the start should be gradual, and parents should remember the child is not obligated to fast.

## Is your child ready? A quick checklist

Use this table before Ramadan begins. If you are ticking mostly “not yet,” keep your child involved in other ways and try again next year.

| Sign | Ready for a short fast | Not yet |
|---|---|---|
| Interest | Asks to fast, talks about it excitedly | Agrees only because siblings do |
| Health | Well, eating normally, no ongoing condition | Recently ill, on medication or has a chronic condition (see a doctor first) |
| Hunger tolerance | Can wait for a late lunch without a meltdown | Gets shaky or very upset when a meal is late |
| Understanding | Can explain in simple words why Muslims fast | Sees it as a contest or a punishment |
| Communication | Will tell you honestly when they feel unwell | Hides how they feel to “win” |

The last row matters most. A child who will hide a headache to keep going is not ready, however keen they are.

## First fast for kids: a step-by-step plan

This plan assumes a healthy child aged around 7 to 10. Younger children can stay on Steps 1 and 2 for a whole Ramadan, or simply join suhoor and iftar without fasting at all.

1. **Step 1: Talk about it a week before Ramadan.** Explain what fasting is, why Allah asks adults to fast, and that children practise in small pieces. Read Surah Al-Baqarah 2:183 together, where Allah says fasting was prescribed “that you may become righteous.”
2. **Step 2: Start with a breakfast-to-lunch fast.** Your child eats a normal breakfast, then fasts until lunch. This is a real achievement for a first attempt and carries very little risk.
3. **Step 3: Try a suhoor-to-noon fast on a weekend.** Wake your child for suhoor, then fast until midday. Weekends work best because you can control activity and rest.
4. **Step 4: Stretch to mid-afternoon.** If noon went well, try a fast until around Asr on the next weekend, then break with dates and water.
5. **Step 5: Try a full-day fast on a quiet day.** Only when shorter fasts feel easy. Choose a weekend or holiday, keep the day calm and plan a nap.
6. **Step 6: Alternate days, not consecutive ones.** For children building up, a fasting day followed by a normal day is gentler than several full fasts in a row.
7. **Step 7: Review together after each fast.** Ask what felt hard, what helped and what they want to try next time. Let your child help set the next goal.

Some families also use set fasting windows, as the Saudi Ministry of Health suggests, such as fasting the first and last days of Ramadan or fasting fixed hours each day. Pick whichever suits your child, and remember that the same guidance says very young children who practise should hold back from food only, not water.

> **Tip:** Write each fast on a simple chart: “Breakfast to lunch,” “Suhoor to noon,” “Suhoor to Asr.” Seeing the ladder climb motivates children far more than one big “full fast” goal.

## Suhoor and iftar for young fasters

What your child eats outside fasting hours decides how the fast feels. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said, “Take suhur as there is a blessing in it” (Sahih al-Bukhari 1923). For children, suhoor is not optional on a fasting day.

Paediatric advice from [Children’s National Hospital](https://riseandshine.childrensnational.org/children-and-ramadan-fasting/) recommends a suhoor built on fibre and protein, and limiting sugary, salty, fried and fizzy foods. Practical suhoor ideas for kids:

- Oatmeal with milk, banana and a spoon of seed butter (QAF is nut-free, but at home use what is safe for your family)
- Eggs with whole-grain toast and cucumber slices
- Labneh or yogurt with whole-grain pita and fruit
- Leftover rice and lentils, which many children happily eat at 5 AM

Offer water steadily between iftar and bedtime, rather than a large amount right before Fajr. At iftar, follow the Sunnah of starting with dates or water. The Prophet broke his fast with fresh dates, or dry dates, or a few sips of water if there were none (Sunan Abi Dawud 2356). Then serve a normal-sized meal, not a feast. Children who overeat after a fast often feel sick.

Teach the dua for breaking the fast:

ذَهَبَ الظَّمَأُ وَابْتَلَّتِ الْعُرُوقُ وَثَبَتَ الأَجْرُ إِنْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ

*Dhahaba az-zama’u wabtallatil-‘urooqu wa thabatal-ajru in sha’ Allah*: “Thirst has gone, the veins are moist, and the reward is sure, if Allah wills” (Sunan Abi Dawud 2357, graded hasan).

## Health and safety: when to stop the fast

Children have less reserve than adults. They are smaller, burn energy quickly and may not notice thirst until they are already low on fluids. Decide in advance that **ending the fast early is a success, not a failure**, and say so to your child.

End the fast and give fluids right away if your child shows signs of dehydration. The Canadian Paediatric Society’s [Caring for Kids](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/health-conditions-and-treatments/dehydration_and_diarrhea) lists signs such as peeing much less than usual, unusual thirst, a dry mouth and tongue, no tears and a faster heartbeat. HealthLinkBC adds dizziness when standing up as a moderate sign.

Stop the fast and seek medical help if your child:

- Feels faint, very weak or too dizzy to stand
- Is confused or unusually hard to wake
- Vomits, or has had very little urine for many hours
- Has a headache that does not ease after resting

For non-urgent questions, Ontario families can call **Health811** (dial 811) to speak to a registered nurse at any hour; see [Ontario’s Health811 page](https://www.ontario.ca/page/your-health). For an emergency, call 911.

**Talk to your child’s doctor before any fast if your child has diabetes, a kidney or heart condition, an eating disorder, low weight, or takes regular medication.** Islam’s rules already exempt the sick, and Allah says in Surah Al-Baqarah 2:185 that He “intends for you ease and does not intend for you hardship.” A child with a health condition can share Ramadan through prayer, Quran and charity instead.

## Fasting on school days

Most children in Ottawa will meet their first fasts on school days in February 2027. For a first attempt, we recommend avoiding school days altogether. If an older child insists:

- Tell the teacher in writing the day before, and ask that your child be allowed to rest or skip intense gym activity.
- Pack a normal lunch anyway. If your child feels unwell, they can eat it without needing to call home first.
- Agree on a clear rule: “If you feel dizzy, you eat. No phone call needed.”
- Keep after-school sports light that week.

Ontario school boards accommodate religious observance; see our guide to [Muslim kids in public school](/blog/muslim-kids-in-public-school) for how to ask for prayer space and Ramadan accommodations.

## Making the first fast meaningful

The memory your child keeps is not the hunger. It is the feeling of being included in something the whole family does for Allah.

- **Make the intention together.** Explain that a fast starts with the heart: “I am fasting this morning for Allah.”
- **Fill the waiting time.** Like the wool toys in Bukhari 1960, plan calm activities for the last hour: a puzzle, a story, helping in the kitchen. Our list of [Ramadan activities for kids](/blog/ramadan-activities-for-kids) has dozens of ideas.
- **Celebrate effort, not hours.** A sticker for each practice fast, a special plate at iftar, or the job of announcing the adhan.
- **Avoid comparison.** Siblings and cousins will fast different amounts. Each child’s ladder is their own.
- **Connect it to the rest of Ramadan.** Older children can look ahead to the last ten nights; our guide to [Laylatul Qadr activities for kids](/blog/laylatul-qadr-for-kids) shows how to make those nights special without exhausting a young faster.

For children aged 8 and up, a weekly class where Ramadan is discussed with peers also helps. [Weekend Islamic schools in Ottawa](/blog/weekend-islamic-schools-ottawa) often cover fasting in their Islamic Studies lessons in the weeks before Ramadan.
