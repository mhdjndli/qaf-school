The child care expense deduction lets Canadian parents deduct money paid to have someone look after their child so they can work, run a business or go to school. For kindergarten, the key question is whether what you paid counts as **child care** or **education**. Before- and after-school care usually qualifies. Kindergarten tuition is less clear-cut, and the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) looks at the facts. This guide explains the general CRA rules, how they apply to JK and SK in Ontario, and what receipts to keep.

> **Key takeaways**
> - The child care expense deduction is claimed on line 21400 of your return, using Form T778. In most two-parent families, the parent with the lower net income must claim it.
> - The CRA allows fees paid to a school only for the part that relates to child care, such as supervision before and after classes or at lunch. Tuition for an educational program is not eligible.
> - For children under compulsory school age (6 in Ontario), the CRA's folio says payments to a school are generally considered child care "unless the facts indicate otherwise."
> - Annual limits are $8,000 for each child under 7 at year-end, and claims cannot exceed two-thirds of the claiming parent's earned income.
> - In Ontario, the same deduction is the basis for the refundable CARE tax credit for families with income of $150,000 or less.

> **Important:** This article is general information based on CRA and Government of Ontario pages as of October 2026. It is not tax advice. Rules and amounts can change, and your situation may differ. Confirm with the [CRA](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/corporate/contact-information.html) or a qualified tax professional before you claim.

## What is the child care expense deduction?

The child care expense deduction is a federal deduction for amounts you, or another person, paid to have someone else look after an **eligible child** so that you could:

- earn employment income,
- carry on a business alone or as an active partner,
- carry on research or similar work under a grant, or
- attend school in a qualifying educational program.

The CRA's [line 21400 page](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/topics/about-your-tax-return/tax-return/completing-a-tax-return/deductions-credits-expenses/line-21400-child-care-expenses.html) is the starting point. An eligible child is generally your child, or your spouse's or common-law partner's child, who was **under 16** at some time in the year, or older and dependent with a mental or physical infirmity.

A deduction reduces your taxable income. It is not a dollar-for-dollar refund. How much tax it saves depends on your income and tax bracket.

## What child care expenses can you claim?

The CRA's page on [expenses you can claim](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/topics/about-your-tax-return/tax-return/completing-a-tax-return/deductions-credits-expenses/line-21400-child-care-expenses/expenses-claim.html) lists payments to:

- caregivers providing child care services,
- day nursery schools and daycare centres,
- **educational institutions, for the part of fees that relate to child care services,**
- day camps and day sports schools whose primary goal is caring for children, and
- boarding schools, overnight sports schools or camps, within weekly limits.

It lists these as **not** eligible: medical or hospital care, clothing, transportation, **education fees such as tuition**, and leisure or recreational fees such as lessons or club registrations.

You also cannot claim amounts that were reimbursed or covered by financial assistance. If the City of Ottawa subsidy pays part of your child care, you can claim only the part you actually paid. Our [Ottawa child care subsidy guide](/blog/ottawa-child-care-subsidy-guide) explains how subsidy works.

## Child care vs education: the rule that matters for kindergarten

This is where kindergarten gets tricky. The CRA's technical guidance, [Income Tax Folio S1-F3-C1](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/technical-information/income-tax/income-tax-folios-index/series-1-individuals/folio-3-family-unit-issues/income-tax-folio-s1-f3-c1-child-care-expense-deduction.html), addresses it directly in paragraph 1.18.

In summary, the folio says:

- An educational program is more goal-oriented than child care. Children are expected to learn skills and progress through a planned program of instruction.
- Where a school provides both child care and an educational program, only the child care portion of the fees, such as supervision before and after classes or during lunch, may qualify.
- When payments are made to an educational institution for a child **under the compulsory school age**, the services are "generally considered child care rather than education unless the facts indicate otherwise."

In Ontario, children are not required to attend school until age 6, and the province describes [kindergarten as optional](https://www.ontario.ca/page/kindergarten). Most JK and SK children are 4 or 5, so they are under compulsory school age.

### What this means in practice

The folio sets a general starting point, not a guarantee. "Unless the facts indicate otherwise" means the CRA can look at what a program actually is. A structured, curriculum-based kindergarten with planned instruction has features of an educational program. A program that is mostly supervision and care looks more like child care.

Tax writers and preparers in Canada do not all read this the same way for private kindergarten tuition. Some treat the full fee for an under-6 child as child care. Others treat curriculum-based tuition as education and claim only separately billed care. Because the outcome depends on the facts, this is exactly the kind of question to confirm with the CRA or a tax professional before you file, especially if the amount is large.

> **Tip:** Ask your school, in writing, how its fees are structured. Is there a separate charge for before-care, after-care or lunch supervision? Will it issue a receipt for child care, and for which amount? Clear paperwork makes any claim easier to support.

## How the rules apply to common kindergarten costs

The table below summarizes how the CRA's general categories usually line up with typical kindergarten-year costs. It is a guide, not a ruling.

| Cost | Generally claimable? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Licensed before- and after-school program | Usually yes | Supervision before and after classes is child care |
| Lunch-hour supervision fee billed separately | Usually yes | Named as child care in the CRA folio |
| Daycare or home child care for a younger sibling | Usually yes | Day nurseries and caregivers are listed |
| Summer or March Break day camp | Usually yes | Day camps whose main purpose is care are listed |
| Private JK/SK fees (child under 6) | It depends | Folio presumes child care for under compulsory age, unless the facts indicate an educational program |
| Private school tuition for Grade 1 and up | Generally no | Education fees and tuition are not eligible |
| Swimming, music or sports lessons | Generally no | Leisure and recreational fees are not eligible |
| Uniforms, supplies, transportation | No | Clothing and transportation are excluded |

## How much can you claim?

The folio sets annual limits per child:

- **$8,000** for each child under 7 at the end of the year,
- **$5,000** for each child aged 7 to 16 (and in some other cases), and
- **$11,000** for each child eligible for the disability tax credit.

Your total claim also cannot exceed **two-thirds of your earned income** for the year, such as salary, wages and active business income. For boarding schools and overnight camps, there are weekly limits based on the annual amount. Check [Form T778](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/forms/t778.html) for the tax year you are filing, since forms are updated every year.

A JK or SK child is under 7 at year-end, so the $8,000 limit generally applies.

## Who in the family claims it?

According to the CRA's page on [who can claim the deduction](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/topics/about-your-tax-return/tax-return/completing-a-tax-return/deductions-credits-expenses/line-21400-child-care-expenses/who-claim-deduction.html), if you live with a spouse or common-law partner, the person with the **lower net income** (including zero income) must generally claim the expenses.

The higher-income person can claim only in specific situations, for example if the lower-income partner was enrolled in a qualifying educational program, was unable to care for children because of an infirmity (with a physician's statement), or was confined to a prison or similar institution for at least two weeks. If net incomes are equal, you agree on who claims.

Single parents who are the only person supporting the child can claim the expenses they paid while the child lived with them.

## Receipts: what to ask your school or provider for

The CRA requires a receipt from the person or organization that provided the care. If the provider is an individual, such as a babysitter or home child care provider, the receipt must show their **social insurance number**.

A good child care receipt includes:

- the provider's name and address,
- the child's name,
- the period of care and the amount paid,
- a description of the services (for example, after-school care), and
- the provider's SIN if the provider is an individual.

You do not send receipts with your return. Keep them in case the CRA asks for them later. Receipts can be in your name or your spouse's or partner's name.

**Payments to relatives:** you cannot claim payments to the child's parent, to someone you claim as a dependant, or to a related person under 18. An adult grandparent or aunt can be a paid caregiver as long as they give you a proper receipt.

## Ontario's CARE credit and donation receipts

### The Ontario CARE tax credit

Ontario families can get extra help through the [Ontario Child Care Tax Credit](https://www.ontario.ca/page/ontario-child-care-tax-credit), also called the CARE credit. It is a refundable credit, so you can receive it even if you owe no tax.

According to the Government of Ontario:

- You must be eligible for the federal child care expense deduction, have family income of **$150,000 or less**, and live in Ontario at the end of the year.
- The credit is a percentage of your deduction, from **75%** for the lowest incomes, declining as income rises, and nothing above $150,000.
- You claim it on Schedule ON479-A with your return.

In other words, getting the federal deduction right also determines your Ontario credit.

### Donation receipts from religious schools

Some families hear that religious school fees can be partly receipted as donations. The CRA's [Information Circular IC75-23](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/publications/ic75-23.html) explains a narrow exception. A school that is a registered charity and provides both academic and religious education may, under certain calculations, issue a donation receipt only for the part of a payment that exceeds the cost per pupil of the academic program.

In practice, this rarely applies to schools that charge set tuition. Do not assume your school can issue one; ask, and check with a tax professional.

## The bottom line

The child care expense deduction can help kindergarten families, but only for real child care costs, backed by proper receipts and claimed by the right parent. Before- and after-school care is usually the clearest claim. Private JK/SK fees for children under 6 fall into a grey area that depends on the facts, so ask your school for clear receipts and confirm with the CRA or a tax professional before you claim.
