Knowing how to choose a weekend Islamic school saves you a year of half-hearted Saturdays. The short answer: decide your child's main goal first, then judge each program on teaching quality, levelling, safety, schedule and how it communicates with parents, and always visit a live class before you register. This checklist walks you through every step, with a printable scorecard and the questions to ask.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Start with your goal: Quran reading, hifz, Arabic, Islamic identity, or a mix. The best school for one goal is not always the best for another.
> - Teacher quality and correct levelling matter more than building, brand or price.
> - Ask about police checks and a written child-safety policy at every program, including volunteer-run ones.
> - Watch a real class: are children reading aloud, engaged and kindly corrected?
> - Calculate cost per hour of teaching and add the drive; a cheaper program across the city can cost more in time.

## What a good weekend Islamic school looks like

A weekend Islamic school is a part-time program, usually on Saturday or Sunday, that teaches Quran, Arabic and Islamic Studies to children who attend a public, Catholic or private school during the week. Good ones share a few traits: children are grouped by actual level, every child reads aloud to a teacher every week, teachers are warm and consistent, and parents know what their child is learning.

Research on these programs, such as the [Institute for Social Policy and Understanding's work on weekend Islamic schools](https://www.ispu.org/weekend-islamic-schools/), focuses on character and identity as well as content. That matches what parents tell us: the best weekend schools leave children *wanting* to come back.

## How to choose a weekend Islamic school in 8 steps

Work through these steps in order. Each one ends with questions you can ask by phone, by email or on a visit.

### Step 1: Decide your child's main goal

Before you compare programs, write one sentence: "This year, I want my child to…" Common answers:

- **Read the Quran fluently** with correct letters and basic tajweed.
- **Start or continue hifz** with steady memorization and revision.
- **Read and write Arabic** as a language, not only for recitation.
- **Build Islamic identity** and friendships with Muslim peers.
- **All of the above, gently**, because they are 8 and new to this.

If your answer is mainly hifz or very targeted correction, compare weekend schools against a tutor or halaqa too; our [Islamic Sunday school vs Quran tutor](/blog/weekend-islamic-school-vs-quran-tutor) comparison helps with that decision.

### Step 2: Check teaching quality

Teachers make or break a weekend school. Many programs depend on volunteers, which can be wonderful, but consistency matters.

**Questions to ask:**

1. Who will teach my child's class, and how long have they taught here?
2. What training do teachers have in Quran, Arabic or teaching children?
3. Is it the same teacher every week, or does it rotate?
4. How do teachers correct mistakes? (Look for patient, specific correction, not shaming.)
5. How many children are in each class, and is there an assistant?

**What to watch for in class:** a teacher who knows every child's name, children reading aloud one at a time, and gentle correction of pronunciation.

### Step 3: Ask how children are levelled

Placing a child in the wrong level is the most common reason weekend school "doesn't work." A 10-year-old who cannot yet join letters will feel lost in a class reading from the Mushaf, and a fluent reader will be bored in a Qaida class.

**Questions to ask:**

1. Is there a placement assessment, and who does it?
2. Are classes grouped by age, by level, or both?
3. Can a child move up mid-year?
4. Which Qaida or textbook series do you use? (Consistency with home practice helps; see [Noorani Qaida vs Qaida Baghdadia](/blog/noorani-qaida-vs-qaida-baghdadia).)
5. How do you support a child who is behind or who learns differently?

### Step 4: Look at the curriculum balance

Weekend time is short, so how it is spent matters. Ask for a typical timetable.

| Subject | Questions that reveal quality |
|---|---|
| Quran | How many minutes does each child recite individually? Is there a memorization plan with revision? |
| Arabic | Is there a progression from letters to words to sentences? Do children write as well as read? |
| Islamic Studies | Does it cover salah, wudu, duas, adab and seerah in age-appropriate ways? Is it mainstream and non-sectarian? |
| Extras | Are there sports, STEM, art or breaks? Children arriving after a full school week need movement. |

For deeper criteria on the language side, see [Arabic school for kids: what to look for](/blog/arabic-school-for-kids-what-to-look-for).

### Step 5: Ask hard questions about safety

Every program that cares for children should be able to answer these clearly, whether it is run by a masjid, a non-profit or a private school.

1. Do all teachers and volunteers have a recent police Vulnerable Sector Check? (Ottawa residents get these through the [Ottawa Police Service](https://www.ottawapolice.ca/en/reports-and-services/police-record-and-background-checks/police-record-checks/).)
2. Is there a written child-safety or safeguarding policy I can read?
3. How are children signed in and released, and who can pick them up?
4. Are two adults present, or are classrooms visible, during lessons?
5. Is there a nut or allergy policy, and how are medical needs handled?
6. What is the discipline approach?

Canada's [Commit to Kids](https://www.commit2kids.ca/en/) program, run by the Canadian Centre for Child Protection, helps faith-based and community organizations build these policies. A program that already uses something similar is a good sign.

> **Tip:** Hesitation or vague answers to safety questions are a red flag, even at a well-loved community program.

### Step 6: Check communication and progress tracking

You should not have to guess what your child learned on Sunday.

**Questions to ask:**

1. How will I know what my child is working on each week?
2. Is there a report card, progress sheet, app or parent portal?
3. What should we practise at home, and for how long?
4. How do I reach the teacher if I have a concern?
5. Are there parent meetings or open days?

Programs that track Quran progress in writing make home practice much easier. At QAF, our Quran and hifz halaqat use a parent portal that shows attendance and hifz progress, so parents can see exactly where their child is.

### Step 7: Do the schedule, travel and cost maths

**Schedule.** A morning program can collide with sports; an afternoon one with family visits. Also check the calendar: does it run September to June, and how does it handle Ramadan, Eid and long weekends?

**Travel.** A weekly 45-minute round trip adds up to more than 25 hours over a school year. Proximity often decides whether a family sticks with a program.

**Cost.** Ask for the total yearly cost, including registration, books and any fundraising expectations. Then divide by the teaching hours. Some programs are very affordable because they rely on volunteers; others charge more for longer hours, smaller classes or extra subjects. Neither is automatically better.

**Free options exist too.** In Ottawa, the OCDSB's [International and Indigenous Languages program](https://www.ocdsb.ca/programs-learning/continuing-education/international-languages-program/international-indigenous-languages-elementary-program) offers Arabic for JK to Grade 8 on Saturday mornings at schools across the city, for a small activity fee for Ontario residents. It teaches Arabic as a language rather than Quran or Islamic Studies, so many families pair it with a masjid program.

### Step 8: Visit, trial and listen to your child

Ask to watch a class, or book a trial day. During the visit:

- Count how many children read aloud in 20 minutes.
- Notice whether children seem happy, bored or anxious.
- Look at the walls and notebooks: is there visible work and progress?
- Ask one or two parents at pick-up what they like and what they would change.

Afterwards, ask your child three questions: What did you learn? Who did you sit with? Do you want to go back? A child's honest reaction after a trial is one of the best predictors of a successful year.

## Red flags to take seriously

- No placement process; all 8 to 12-year-olds in one group regardless of level.
- Teachers change most weeks with no handover.
- No answer, or an irritated answer, to safety questions.
- Children sit for hours with no break or movement.
- Harsh discipline, shaming or comparing children publicly.
- No information on what was taught or what to practise.

## Final word

Picking a weekend Islamic school is not about finding a perfect program; it is about finding one that matches your child's goal, places them at the right level, keeps them safe and tells you how they are doing. Use the scorecard, visit in person, and trust what your child tells you after the trial.
