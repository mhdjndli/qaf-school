## The best Islamic cartoons and channels for kids

### 1. Omar & Hana (Free on YouTube)

**What it is:** A Malaysian animated series from Digital Durian, the team behind Durioo, following siblings Omar and Hana through everyday family life. It ran for five seasons and 117 episodes, and was released in English, Malay, Arabic, Urdu and other languages, according to its Wikipedia entry. Its English YouTube channel has around 4 million subscribers.

**Best for:** Toddlers and preschoolers learning first duas, Islamic phrases and manners through songs.

**Ages and price:** Made for ages 2 to 6. Free on [the official YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UC178EmfQAV3OT-UpuO6WUMg); the full catalogue is also on Durioo+.

**Our take:** The gold standard for little ones. Episodes are short, warm and easy to connect to real life ("Remember what Omar said before eating?"). Many of its songs use instrumental music, so if your family avoids instruments, use the Durioo+ vocal-only filter instead.

### 2. Durioo+ (Free tier, paid plans)

**What it is:** The streaming app from the makers of Omar & Hana. Alongside Omar & Hana, the [Durioo website](https://durioo.com/) features newer originals including Little Ammar, Mina & Mila, ILM and Sirah Nabawiyyah. The App Store listing says every video is reviewed by a content team and a Shariah consultant.

**Best for:** Families who want one curated space with parental controls, including daily screen-time limits, child profiles by age and offline downloads.

**Ages and price:** Durioo markets its characters for ages 2 to 8. There is a free tier; at the time of writing, the Canadian App Store lists paid plans at $17.99 a month or $116.99 a year, with special offers that change often.

**Our take:** The **vocal-only filter** and a switch to hide fast-paced videos are standout features for parents who care about music and overstimulation. Compare the price against how much your child actually watches.

### 3. Zaky & Friends by One4Kids (Free on YouTube)

**What it is:** A long-running series starring Zaky, a friendly bear, and his friends, teaching duas, Quran and the names of Allah through stories and nasheeds. The One4kids YouTube channel (@One4kids-Zaky) has more than 1.6 million subscribers.

**Best for:** Families who prefer music-free content. The publisher describes the One4Kids TV app's content as "carefully designed to be music-free," using vocals and nasheeds without instruments.

**Ages and price:** Preschool to early elementary. Free on YouTube; the One4Kids TV app is listed at $9.99 a month or $99.99 a year in the Canadian App Store.

**Our take:** Slower and simpler than Omar & Hana, which many parents of two- to four-year-olds see as a plus. Good for learning specific duas and surahs by repetition.

### 4. Muslim Kids TV (Subscription)

**What it is:** A subscription streaming service from Milo Productions offering, by its own count, more than 15,000 resources: Islamic shows, movies, games, eBooks and a "Recitation buddy" for Quran.

**Best for:** Families with children of different ages who want a large library on the TV as well as tablets.

**Ages and price:** Content runs from preschool to preteen. The [Muslim Kids TV website](https://www.muslimkids.tv/) lists $9.99 a month or $79.99 a year, with a 14-day free trial. It works on the web, iOS, Android, Roku, Apple TV, Amazon Fire, LG, Samsung and Android TV.

**Our take:** The broadest catalogue on this list, which is both its strength and its challenge. Set up profiles and preview shows, because a big library includes a wider range of styles than a single series.

### 5. IQRA Cartoon (Free)

**What it is:** A non-profit animation studio that makes short, fully animated videos telling the stories of the prophets and the Quran. Its main [YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCSL4Dn-FQlvHZiSKipQZMqw) has roughly 480,000 subscribers and close to 300 videos.

**Best for:** Children ready for real stories: Prophet Yunus, Musa, Ibrahim, the Companions and the meanings behind familiar surahs.

**Ages and price:** Best for about 5 to 10. Free; supported by donations.

**Our take:** Ideal for the age when children start asking "What happened next?" Some stories include tense moments (floods, battles, the sea parting), so watch the first few together with younger children.

### 6. Nussa (Free on YouTube)

**What it is:** A popular Indonesian animated series from Visinema Studios about Nussa, his little sister Rarra and their family's everyday Islamic life. The official NussaOfficial channel has more than 12 million subscribers and is still releasing new stories.

**Best for:** School-age children who enjoy longer, story-driven episodes about family, honesty and kindness.

**Ages and price:** About 4 to 10. Free.

**Our take:** Beautifully animated, with themes that translate across cultures. Because the series is produced in Indonesia, check the language and subtitles of each video before handing it to a pre-reader.

### 7. Aya and Yusuf (Free on YouTube)

**What it is:** An animated series from Aya Animations about six-year-old twins exploring the countryside where they live. According to Islam Channel, it teaches honesty, gratitude, helping others, caring for the environment and appreciating Allah's creation, with lessons drawn from the Quran.

**Best for:** Calm, nature-focused viewing for young children.

**Ages and price:** Made for ages 2 to 8. Free on its YouTube channel; it has also aired on IC Kids on Islam Channel.

**Our take:** A newer, smaller series with a gentle pace. It pairs naturally with nature walks and conversations about Allah as the Creator.

### 8. Noor Kids (Books, not a cartoon)

**What it is:** Not a cartoon at all, but worth including. Noor Kids is a subscription of illustrated Islamic storybooks with recurring characters, a parent's guide and a sticker "passport," covering character (akhlaq), beliefs (aqeedah) and citizenship. It also runs online courses for older children.

**Best for:** Families who want the appeal of familiar characters without more screen time.

**Ages and price:** Mainly early elementary. Pricing varies with promotions, so check the [Noor Kids website](https://noorkids.com/cbp).

**Our take:** A good "instead of a screen" option for car rides, waiting rooms and bedtime. For more ideas, see our list of the [best Islamic books for kids](/blog/best-islamic-books-for-kids).

## How to choose an Islamic cartoon for your child

A show being labelled "Islamic" doesn't automatically make it right for your child. Before adding one to your rotation, watch an episode yourself and ask:

- **Is the religious content accurate?** Duas, Quran recitation and stories should match what your child learns at home and school. If something seems off, check it against Quran.com or Sunnah.com, or ask a teacher.
- **Is it paced for my child's age?** Fast cuts and constant noise overstimulate toddlers. Slower shows with simple dialogue are better for under-fives.
- **Does it model good adab?** Look for characters who say salam, share, apologise and respect parents, not just characters who recite.
- **Are there ads or autoplay?** Free YouTube viewing can drift into unrelated videos. A dedicated app, or YouTube Kids with hand-approved content, keeps you in control.
- **What language is it in?** Arabic-language shows can support Arabic learning. Our guide to the [best Arabic apps for kids](/blog/best-arabic-apps-for-kids) covers language-focused options.

## A note on music, images and differing opinions

Muslim families make different choices about cartoons. Scholars hold a range of views on musical instruments, and some discourage animated content for children altogether. Others consider wholesome, values-based cartoons a reasonable part of a child's media diet.

This list doesn't settle that question for you. If music matters to your family, choose One4Kids or use Durioo+'s vocal-only filter. If you prefer to avoid animation, Noor Kids books, audio Quran and nasheeds are good alternatives. For guidance specific to your family, speak with a scholar or imam you trust.

## Free YouTube or a paid app?

**Free YouTube** gives you Omar & Hana, Zaky, IQRA Cartoon, Nussa and Aya and Yusuf at no cost. The trade-offs are ads, autoplay and recommended videos that may not match your values. If you use YouTube, use the YouTube Kids app and set it to show only content you've approved.

**Paid apps** (Durioo+, Muslim Kids TV, One4Kids TV) cost money but give you a curated environment, child profiles and, in some cases, built-in time limits. For families whose children watch a few times a week, the free route with careful settings is often enough. For daily viewing, a paid app usually pays for itself in peace of mind.

Either way, the device settings matter as much as the show. Our guide to [parental controls for Muslim families](/blog/parental-controls-muslim-families) walks through YouTube Kids, iPad and Android settings step by step.

## Screen-time tips for Muslim families

The Canadian Paediatric Society's guidance on [screen use and young children](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/behavior-and-development/screen-time-and-young-children) recommends no routine screen use for children under 2 (video chats with family are the exception) and about an hour or less a day of sedentary screen time for children aged 2 to 5. It also suggests watching with your child, keeping meals and the hour before bed screen-free, and choosing educational, age-appropriate content.

Here's how that looks in a Muslim home:

1. **Step 1: Set a predictable window.** For example, one episode after Asr or while dinner is being made, not "whenever they ask."
2. **Step 2: Pause for salah.** When the adhan goes, the screen goes off. This teaches children that prayer comes first.
3. **Step 3: Watch together when you can.** Co-viewing turns a cartoon into a conversation.
4. **Step 4: Keep bedtime screen-free.** Replace the last hour with a story, the bedtime duas and Al-Ikhlas, Al-Falaq and An-Nas.
5. **Step 5: Balance screens with real experiences.** Every episode about gratitude should be matched by real moments of saying Alhamdulillah together.

> **Tip:** Keep devices in shared spaces like the living room rather than bedrooms, and use the device's own timer so the screen, not you, says "time's up."

## Turning watching into learning

The best Islamic cartoons for kids are a starting point. What makes the lesson stick is what happens after the screen goes off:

- **Ask one question.** "Why did Hana say sorry?" or "What did Prophet Yunus say in the whale?"
- **Act it out.** Children love replaying a scene with stuffed animals, which helps them remember it.
- **Use the dua in real life.** If the episode taught the dua for leaving home, say it together on your next outing. Our list of [daily duas for kids](/blog/daily-duas-for-kids) has the Arabic, transliteration and sources.
- **Connect it to Allah.** A cartoon about rain is a chance to talk about Allah sending it. Our guide to [teaching kids about Allah](/blog/teaching-kids-about-allah) has more ideas.
