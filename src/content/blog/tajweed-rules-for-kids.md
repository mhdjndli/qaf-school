Tajweed rules for kids are the handful of pronunciation rules a child needs to recite the Quran correctly: where letters come from, which letters are heavy, the nasal ghunnah, the bounce of qalqalah, the four noon sakinah rules, the three meem sakinah rules, sun and moon letters, and how long to stretch each madd. Every rule below is explained in plain words with an example your child already knows from Juz Amma, following the standard Hafs ‘an ‘Asim recitation used in most mushafs around the world.

You do not need to be a qualified teacher to use this guide. You do need a good listener for your child, ideally a teacher, because tajweed is learned by ear before it is learned from a book.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Tajweed means reciting each letter correctly, with its proper qualities, as the Quran was taught by the Prophet ﷺ.
> - Young children (4–6) learn tajweed by imitation; rule names can wait until about age 7–9.
> - Teach one rule at a time and practise it in surahs your child has memorized.
> - The most important early goal is avoiding clear mistakes that change a letter or vowel.
> - Every example below is from Surah Al-Fatiha or Juz Amma, so your child can practise in salah.

## What tajweed means, and why it matters

**Tajweed** (تجويد) comes from an Arabic root meaning “to make something good” or “to improve it.” In Quran recitation, it means giving every letter its correct point of articulation and its qualities, and applying the rules of joining, stretching and stopping. Allah says:

أَوْ زِدْ عَلَيْهِ وَرَتِّلِ ٱلْقُرْءَانَ تَرْتِيلًا

“Or add to it, and recite the Qur’an with measured recitation” ([Surah Al-Muzzammil 73:4](https://quran.com/73/4)).

Scholars of recitation distinguish between two kinds of mistakes. **Clear mistakes** (al-lahn al-jali) change a letter, a vowel or a word, and must be avoided. **Subtle mistakes** (al-lahn al-khafi) affect the polish of recitation, such as a ghunnah held too short. With children, start by removing clear mistakes, then polish.

Encourage a child who finds this hard. The Prophet ﷺ said that one who is proficient in the Quran is with the noble, upright recording angels, and the one who falters in it and finds it difficult has two rewards ([Sahih Muslim 798a](https://sunnah.com/muslim:798a)).

## The best age to start learning tajweed

The best age to start tajweed is as soon as your child begins reciting, but how you teach it changes with age.

| Age | How tajweed is learned | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| 4–6 | Imitation: listen and repeat after a teacher or reciter | Correct sounds without rule names |
| 7–9 | Simple rule names, colours and hand signals | Can spot ghunnah, qalqalah, madd in a mushaf |
| 10–12 | Full beginner rule set, with reasons | Applies noon/meem sakinah and madd rules reliably |
| 13+ | Classical texts and detailed rules | May study texts like *Tuhfat al-Atfal* with a qualified teacher |

At QAF’s [Islamic kindergarten in Ottawa](/program), for example, four- and five-year-olds learning short surahs from Juz Amma are taught to copy the teacher’s sounds exactly. They are not taught terms like “ikhfa”; they simply hear and repeat the correct sound until it becomes natural.

## Rule 1: Say each letter from its correct place (makharij)

Every Arabic letter has a **makhraj**, the exact place in the throat, tongue, lips or nose where the sound is made. Most clear mistakes in children’s recitation are makhraj mistakes: ح (ha from the middle of the throat) said like ه (ha from the deepest part of the throat), ع said like ء, ق said like ك, or ث said like س.

This is a big topic, so we gave it its own guide: [makharij for kids](/blog/makharij-for-kids) explains the five main areas with simple mouth exercises. If your child is still learning the alphabet, a primer such as the Noorani Qaida builds this foundation; see [Noorani Qaida vs Qaida Baghdadia](/blog/noorani-qaida-vs-qaida-baghdadia).

## Rule 2: Heavy and light letters

Some letters are always pronounced **heavy** (tafkhim), with the back of the tongue raised so the sound is full and deep. These seven letters are often memorized with the phrase **خُصَّ ضَغْطٍ قِظْ**:

خ ص ض غ ط ق ظ

Your child can hear the heavy ق at the start of Surah Al-Qari‘ah: “ٱلْقَارِعَةُ” (101:1).

Two other letters are sometimes heavy and sometimes light:

- **The lam in the name “Allah”** is heavy after a fatha or damma and light after a kasra. Surah An-Nasr shows both: “نَصْرُ ٱللَّهِ” (110:1) is heavy, while “دِينِ ٱللَّهِ” (110:2) is light. In “بِسْمِ ٱللَّهِ” (1:1) it is also light.
- **The letter ra (ر)** is generally heavy with a fatha or damma, as in “رَبُّكَ” (105:1), and light with a kasra, as in “مِن شَرِّ” (113:2). A ra with sukun has further rules your child’s teacher will explain later.

## Rule 3: Ghunnah, the nasal sound

**Ghunnah** is a humming sound from the nose. It is held for **two counts** whenever noon (ن) or meem (م) has a shaddah.

إِنَّآ أَعْطَيْنَـٰكَ ٱلْكَوْثَرَ

In “إِنَّآ” (108:1) your child should hold the nasal “nn” for two counts before moving on. You can hear it again in “ٱلنَّاسِ” (114:1).

**A quick test:** ask your child to gently pinch their nose while saying the ghunnah. If the sound stops, the ghunnah is coming from the right place.

## Rule 4: Qalqalah, the bounce

**Qalqalah** is a small echo or bounce on five letters when they have a sukun. The five letters are collected in the phrase **قُطْبُ جَدٍّ**:

ق ط ب ج د

لَمْ يَلِدْ وَلَمْ يُولَدْ

In Surah Al-Ikhlas 112:3, the د in “يَلِدْ” has a light bounce in the middle of the ayah, and the د in “يُولَدْ” has a stronger bounce because you stop on it. Stopping on “ٱلْفَلَقِ” at the end of 113:1 also gives a clear qalqalah on the ق.

Children love this rule. A common mistake is adding a full vowel (“yalida”) instead of a short bounce; listen for that.

## Rule 5: Noon sakinah and tanween

This is the rule set most children are tested on. A **noon sakinah** is a noon with a sukun (نْ). **Tanween** is the doubled vowel at the end of a word (ـً ـٍ ـٌ), which sounds like an “n.” Both follow the same four rules, depending on the next letter.

| Rule | Meaning | Letters that follow | Juz Amma example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Izhar | Say the n clearly | ء ه ع ح غ خ (throat letters) | “مِّنْ خَوْفٍۭ” (106:4) |
| Idgham | Merge the n into the next letter | ي ر م ل و ن | “مِّن مَّسَدٍۭ” (111:5) |
| Iqlab | Turn the n into a hidden m | ب | “لَيُنۢبَذَنَّ” (104:4) |
| Ikhfa | Hide the n with ghunnah | the other 15 letters | “مِن شَرِّ” (113:2) |

### Izhar (clear)

Before the six throat letters, pronounce the noon clearly with no extra ghunnah. In “مِّنْ خَوْفٍۭ” (106:4) the noon is followed by خ, so it is said clearly. The tanween in “كُفُوًا أَحَدٌۢ” (112:4) is followed by a hamza, so it is also clear.

### Idgham (merging)

Before the letters of **يَرْمَلُونَ** (ي ر م ل و ن), the noon merges into the next letter. There are two kinds:

- **With ghunnah** before ي ن م و. Example: “حَبْلٌ مِّن مَّسَدٍۭ” (111:5), where both the tanween and the noon merge into meem with a two-count ghunnah.
- **Without ghunnah** before ل and ر. Example: “وَلَمْ يَكُن لَّهُۥ” (112:4), where the noon disappears into the lam; and “وَيْلٌ لِّكُلِّ” (104:1).

Idgham applies only between two words. Inside a single word, such as “ٱلدُّنْيَا,” the noon stays clear.

### Iqlab (changing)

Before ب, the noon or tanween changes into a hidden meem sound with ghunnah. Most mushafs mark this with a small meem (ۢ) above the noon, as in “لَيُنۢبَذَنَّ” (104:4).

### Ikhfa (hiding)

Before the remaining 15 letters, the noon is “hidden”: the tongue does not fully touch the place of the noon, and the sound passes through the nose with a two-count ghunnah, shaped toward the next letter. Examples: “مِن شَرِّ” (113:2) and “مِّن جُوعٍ” (106:4).

## Rule 6: Meem sakinah

A **meem sakinah** (مْ) has three rules, all with a lip sound, which is why they are called *shafawi* (of the lips).

| Rule | Next letter | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ikhfa shafawi | ب | Meem is lightly hidden with ghunnah | “تَرْمِيهِم بِحِجَارَةٍ” (105:4) |
| Idgham shafawi | م | Two meems merge with ghunnah | “وَءَامَنَهُم مِّنْ” (106:4) |
| Izhar shafawi | Any other letter | Meem said clearly | “أَلَمْ تَرَ” (105:1) |

[Surah Quraysh 106:4](https://quran.com/106/4) is a perfect practice ayah: it contains idgham shafawi twice (“أَطْعَمَهُم مِّن” and “وَءَامَنَهُم مِّنْ”), ikhfa (“مِّن جُوعٍ”) and izhar (“مِّنْ خَوْفٍۭ”).

ٱلَّذِىٓ أَطْعَمَهُم مِّن جُوعٍ وَءَامَنَهُم مِّنْ خَوْفٍۭ

Take extra care with izhar shafawi before و and ف, where children often blur the meem.

## Rule 7: Sun letters and moon letters

When a word starts with “al-” (ٱلْ), the lam is either pronounced or silent depending on the next letter.

- **Moon letters (qamariyyah):** the lam is pronounced. “وَٱلْقَمَرِ” (91:2) is said *wal-qamar*.
- **Sun letters (shamsiyyah):** the lam is silent and the next letter is doubled. “وَٱلشَّمْسِ” (91:1) is said *wash-shams*.

وَٱلشَّمْسِ وَضُحَىٰهَا ۝ وَٱلْقَمَرِ إِذَا تَلَىٰهَا

Surah Ash-Shams makes this easy to remember: the sun and the moon appear in the first two ayat. In the mushaf, a sun letter has a shaddah after the lam, and a moon letter has a sukun on the lam.

## Rule 8: Madd, how long to stretch

**Madd** means lengthening a vowel. Children count on their fingers, with one count roughly the time it takes to fold one finger at a steady pace.

| Type | When it happens | Length in Hafs | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural madd | Alif after fatha, waw sakin after damma, ya sakin after kasra | 2 counts | “قُلْ أَعُوذُ” (113:1) |
| Connected madd (muttasil) | Madd letter followed by hamza in the same word | 4 or 5 counts | “جَآءَ” (110:1) |
| Separated madd (munfasil) | Madd letter at the end of a word, hamza at the start of the next | 4 or 5 counts | “إِنَّآ أَعْطَيْنَـٰكَ” (108:1) |
| Necessary madd (lazim) | Madd letter followed by a shaddah or permanent sukun | 6 counts | “ٱلضَّآلِّينَ” (1:7), “ٱلطَّآمَّةُ” (79:34) |
| Madd for stopping (‘arid) | Madd letter before the last letter when you stop | 2, 4 or 6 counts | Stopping on “ٱلرَّحِيمِ” (1:1) |

The lengths above follow Hafs ‘an ‘Asim by way of ash-Shatibiyyah, the path taught in most classes and printed mushafs. Some teachers use other valid paths with small differences, so follow your child’s teacher.

Two habits matter most. First, **be consistent**: if your child stretches the connected madd for four counts, they should do so throughout the recitation. Second, **do not stretch short vowels**. Turning a short fatha into an alif is a clear mistake because it can change the word.

The wavy line above a letter (ٓ) in most mushafs signals a madd longer than two counts, which is a helpful visual cue for children.

## Rule 9: Stopping correctly (waqf)

When you stop at the end of an ayah, the last letter takes a sukun. Two changes are worth teaching early:

- **Tanween with fatha becomes a long “aa.”** Stopping on “أَفْوَاجًا” (110:2) sounds like *afwaajaa*.
- **Ta marbuta (ة) becomes a soft “h.”** Stopping on “ٱلْحُطَمَةِ” (104:4) sounds like *al-hutamah*.

Mushafs also have small stop signs. In common Madani mushafs: **م** means you must stop, **لا** means do not stop here, **ج** means stopping or continuing are both fine, **صلى** means continuing is better, and **قلى** means stopping is better. Teach these one at a time as they come up in your child’s surahs.

## Teaching tajweed rules for kids at home

**Step 1: Pick one rule a week.** Start with ghunnah or qalqalah: they are easy to hear and fun to practise.

**Step 2: Hear it first.** Play a slow recitation of a surah your child knows and ask them to raise a hand every time they hear the rule.

**Step 3: Find it in the mushaf.** Once your child reads, let them hunt for the rule with a coloured pencil on a printed page (never mark a mushaf itself unless it is a study copy intended for that).

**Step 4: Practise in one ayah.** Use the examples above. Surah Quraysh 106:4 and Surah Al-Ikhlas 112:3–4 cover many rules in very few words.

**Step 5: Recite to a listener.** Tajweed only improves with correction. A teacher should hear your child at least weekly. The Prophet ﷺ said: “Beautify the Qur’an with your voices” ([Sunan Abi Dawud 1468](https://sunnah.com/abudawud:1468)), and correct tajweed is the foundation of a beautiful recitation.

Tajweed can sound intimidating, with its Arabic terms and tables, but for a child it is simply learning to say Allah’s words the way they were taught.
