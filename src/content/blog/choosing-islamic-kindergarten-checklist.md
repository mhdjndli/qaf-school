How to choose an Islamic kindergarten comes down to scoring each school on the same handful of criteria: the quality of its Quran, Arabic and Islamic program, how it teaches young children, who the teachers are, how safe and well run it is, and whether the cost and logistics work for your family. This guide gives you a 10-criterion scorecard with suggested weights, explains what "good" looks like for each, and lists the deal-breakers that should rule a school out regardless of score.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Score every school on the same 10 criteria, from 1 to 5, and weight the ones that matter most to your family.
> - A strong Islamic kindergarten balances faith and early learning: daily Quran and Arabic and a play-rich, hands-on program aligned with Ontario's kindergarten curriculum.
> - Ontario does not inspect private elementary schools, so teachers, safety checks and written policies need your direct attention.
> - Some issues are deal-breakers whatever the score: no criminal reference checks, no written fees or contract, or a room where you would not leave your child.
> - Logistics count. A great school that is 50 minutes away may not be the right school.

## How to choose an Islamic kindergarten in 6 steps

**Step 1: Write your top three goals.** For example: "speaks basic Arabic," "knows 10 short surahs and daily duas," "is ready for Grade 1."

**Step 2: Shortlist two to four schools** within a realistic daily drive. Our guide to [Islamic schools in Ottawa](/blog/islamic-schools-in-ottawa-guide) is a good starting point locally.

**Step 3: Visit each one during class time** with the same list of questions. Our [40 questions to ask on a school tour](/blog/questions-to-ask-islamic-school-tour) covers what to ask.

**Step 4: Score each school** using the scorecard below, the same day as your visit.

**Step 5: Check the deal-breakers.** Any one of them rules a school out.

**Step 6: Compare total scores, then sleep on it.** If two schools are close, choose the one where your child seemed happiest.

## The Islamic kindergarten scorecard

Score each criterion from **1 (poor) to 5 (excellent)**, then multiply by the weight. The weights below are a starting point; adjust them to your priorities. The maximum score is 100.

| 6 | Safety and health | Reference checks, allergy plans, secure pick-up, clean space | ×2 | | |

Total weights add up to 20, so 20 × 5 = 100. A school scoring 80 or more is a strong candidate. Below 60, keep looking, unless the low scores are in areas that do not matter to you.

## Criteria 1 and 2: Quran, Islamic Studies and Arabic

This is why you are looking at an Islamic kindergarten, so look closely.

**For Quran and Islamic Studies, a strong program has:**

- Daily Quran time, focused on short surahs from Juz Amma at this age, with correct pronunciation.
- Duas woven into the day: before eating, after eating, entering and leaving.
- Adab taught through routine: salam, sharing, tidying up, kindness.
- Simple, joyful stories of the Prophets and about Allah, not fear-based teaching.
- A mainstream, non-sectarian approach that respects families who differ on minor issues.

**For Arabic, ask how often your child will hear and use it.** A 30-minute weekly lesson and daily immersion are very different. In an immersion setting, Arabic is used for greetings, instructions, songs and routines, so children absorb it naturally. Our post on [why early Arabic immersion matters](/blog/why-early-arabic-immersion-matters) explains why the age-4-to-5 window is valuable.

**Score low if** Quran is only rote repetition with no attention to pronunciation or meaning, or if there is no way to tell you what your child has learned.

## Criterion 3: The early learning approach

A good Islamic kindergarten is first a good kindergarten. Children aged 4 and 5 learn best through hands-on activity, play, conversation and movement.

Since September 2026, Ontario kindergarten classes follow *The Ontario Curriculum, Kindergarten, 2026* (see [Ontario's kindergarten page](https://www.ontario.ca/page/kindergarten)), organized around four strands: Belonging and Contributing, Self-Regulation and Well-Being, Foundations of Language and Mathematics, and Problem Solving and Innovating. Private schools are not required to follow it, but a school that aligns with it makes the move to Grade 1 easier.

Look for:

- A clear daily rhythm with long stretches of focused, child-chosen work.
- Real materials children can handle: counting objects, letter shapes, puzzles, practical-life activities.
- Early literacy and numeracy built in, not left out.
- Little time spent on worksheets or sitting still.

If you are comparing styles, our guides to [Montessori meets Islamic values](/blog/montessori-meets-islamic-values) and [play-based vs academic kindergarten](/blog/play-based-vs-academic-kindergarten) help.

## Criterion 4: Teachers

Teachers matter more than buildings. Ontario's [private schools page](https://www.ontario.ca/page/private-schools) notes that private school teachers do not need to be members of the Ontario College of Teachers, so ask directly about qualifications, training and experience.

**What scores well:**

- Teachers with early childhood or teaching credentials, or clear, relevant training.
- Quran teachers with sound recitation and, ideally, formal tajweed training.
- Staff who have been at the school for more than a year or two.
- A warm, calm tone with children. Watch how a teacher handles a child who is upset.

Young children learn through back-and-forth interaction with responsive adults, which Harvard's Center on the Developing Child calls [serve and return](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/science/key-concepts/serve-and-return/). The teacher's warmth is not a soft extra; it is how learning happens.

## Criteria 5 to 8: Class size, safety, communication and environment

### Class size and attention

Smaller groups usually mean more conversation and quicker help. Ask how many children are in the class and how many adults. Our article on [small class size benefits](/blog/small-class-size-benefits) explains what research says and when it matters most.

### Safety and health

Ask about criminal reference checks for all staff and volunteers, allergy and medication plans, first aid, emergency procedures and who is allowed to pick up. Ask whether the school is nut-free and how food is handled. Ontario's ministry suggests parents ask private schools about reference checks and liability insurance before signing.

### Communication

You should know how your child's day went without having to chase anyone. Look for regular updates (an app, photos or notes), an easy way to message the teacher, and written progress reports.

### Environment

Look for a clean, bright, organized room, child-sized furniture, materials on open shelves and children's own work on the walls. Ask how often children go outside.

## Criteria 9 and 10: Logistics and cost

### Logistics

Check the daily hours, drop-off and pick-up windows, the yearly calendar and whether before- or after-care is offered. Then drive the route at rush hour. A school that adds 90 minutes to your day will wear on everyone by February.

### Cost and transparency

Ask for the **total yearly cost** in writing: tuition, registration, resource and activity fees. Ask about sibling discounts, payment schedules and the refund policy if you withdraw. Our breakdown of [Ottawa Islamic school fees](/blog/ottawa-islamic-school-fees-explained) explains what to look for. If you are still deciding whether tuition makes sense at all, read [is Islamic school worth it?](/blog/is-islamic-school-worth-it).

## Deal-breakers: rule a school out if…

Whatever the total score, think twice if:

- The school cannot confirm criminal reference checks for staff.
- There is no written fee schedule, contract or refund policy.
- You are not allowed to see a class in session.
- The room feels unsafe, chaotic or dirty.
- Children seem afraid of adults, or adults speak to children harshly.
- Your questions are met with defensiveness rather than answers.

## A worked example

Say you visit two schools. School A scores 5 on Quran (×3 = 15) but 2 on logistics (×2 = 4) because it is 45 minutes away. School B scores 4 on Quran (12) and 5 on logistics (10). Across all 10 criteria, School A totals 78 and School B totals 84.

School B wins on paper. If your top goal is Quran and you can manage the drive, you might still choose School A. The scorecard does not make the decision for you. It makes the trade-offs visible.

## Conclusion

Knowing how to choose an Islamic kindergarten is mostly about being consistent: the same goals, the same questions and the same scorecard for every school. Weight what matters to you, rule out any school with a deal-breaker, and let your child's reaction on the visit break a tie.
