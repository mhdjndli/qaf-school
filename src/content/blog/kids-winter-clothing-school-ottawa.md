Winter clothes for kindergarten in Ottawa come down to one rule: your child should be able to play outside for a full recess and come back in warm and dry. That means a waterproof snowsuit, insulated boots, two pairs of mitts, a warm hat and a neck warmer, all labelled and easy enough for a four-year-old to put on with little help. Here is exactly what to buy, how to size it, and how to teach your child to dress alone.

> **Key takeaways**
> - The Ottawa kindergarten winter kit: **snow pants and coat (or a one-piece snowsuit), waterproof boots, two pairs of mitts, a toque, a neck warmer** and a spare set of dry clothes.
> - Choose **mittens over gloves** and a **neck warmer over a scarf**, as the Canadian Paediatric Society recommends.
> - **Size boots with room for a thick sock** and wiggling toes, and check the fit again in January.
> - The Canadian Paediatric Society suggests considering keeping children indoors when the temperature or wind chill is **-27°C or colder**. Schools set their own rules, so ask yours.
> - **Practise dressing at home** in a set order. Independence matters more than any brand.

## Why Ottawa winters need a plan

Ottawa winters are long. In most years there is snow on the ground from December into March, along with freezing rain, slush and very cold, windy mornings. Kindergarten children in Ontario usually play outside most days, so your child may dress and undress for outdoors several times a day.

The goal is not to bundle your child so heavily they cannot move. The Canadian Paediatric Society notes that [overdressing makes children sweat](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/safety-and-injury-prevention/winter_safety), and damp children feel colder once they stop playing. Warm, dry and able to run is the target.

## Snowsuit: one-piece or two-piece?

A **two-piece set** (bib snow pants plus a coat) is the most common choice for kindergarten. Bib pants keep snow out of the back, and the coat can be worn alone on milder days. Two pieces are also easier for toileting, which matters for a four-year-old who waits until the last second.

A **one-piece snowsuit** is the warmest option and keeps snow out completely. It can suit a younger JK child who is not yet managing two separate pieces, but taking it off quickly for the bathroom is harder.

**Our take:** For most JK and SK children, bib snow pants and a separate coat strike the best balance of warmth and independence.

### Fit tips

- Buy room for one layer underneath, not two sizes up. A suit that is too big lets snow in and makes climbing awkward.
- Check that the cuffs go **over** the boots, not inside them.
- Test the zipper together. Add a large zipper pull or a loop of ribbon if the tab is tiny.
- Choose bright colours or reflective trim for dark mornings and afternoons.

## Boots that keep feet warm and dry

Cold, wet feet end a child's recess faster than anything else. The Canadian Paediatric Society recommends warm, waterproof boots with room for an extra pair of socks and for toes to wiggle.

- **Waterproof lower shell** (rubber or similar) for Ottawa slush.
- **Removable liner,** which you can pull out and dry overnight.
- **Velcro straps or a wide opening** your child can manage alone.
- **Grippy soles** for icy schoolyards.

Check fit at the start of winter and again in January. Children's feet grow quickly, and a tight boot is a cold boot.

## Mitts, hats and neck warmers

### Mittens, not gloves

Mittens keep fingers together and warmer, and are far easier for small hands. Look for **waterproof mitts with long cuffs** that pull over the coat sleeve. Send **two pairs**: one pair will be wet by lunch. Mitten clips are fine; avoid string, which can catch on equipment.

### A hat that covers the ears

A snug toque that covers the ears matters on windy days. Ears are among the first places to get frostbitten.

### Neck warmer instead of a scarf

Scarves can catch on slides and climbers. The Canadian Paediatric Society advises a **neck warmer instead of a scarf**, and removing drawstrings from hoods and jackets for young children.

### For girls who wear hijab

Some young girls like to wear hijab at school. A thin, pull-on sports-style hijab fits comfortably under a toque, and a fleece neck warmer pulled over it adds warmth without pins. Avoid pins and long loose ends during outdoor play.

## Layers under the snowsuit

Dress your child in normal school clothes plus one warm layer, such as a fleece or long-sleeve top. On very cold days, add thermal leggings under pants.

- **Avoid cotton socks and cotton long underwear** next to the skin on very cold days. They stay wet once damp.
- **Skip bulky sweaters** that make the coat hard to zip.
- Remember the classroom is warm. Your child needs to be comfortable indoors for most of the day.

## How to teach your child to dress alone

Four- and five-year-olds can learn to put on most winter gear themselves, and teachers will thank you. Practise at home on weekends, then on school mornings.

Step 1: **Snow pants first,** while sitting on the floor. Pull the straps over the shoulders.

Step 2: **Boots next.** Pull snow pant cuffs down over the boots.

Step 3: **Coat on with the "flip trick."** Lay the coat open on the floor, hood near your child's feet. Arms go in the sleeves, then flip the coat up over the head.

Step 4: **Zip up.** Hook the zipper together for your child at first, then let them pull.

Step 5: **Neck warmer and toque.**

Step 6: **Mitts last,** with the cuffs over the sleeves.

Make it a game: race the timer, or sing a song from start to finish. Expect it to take 10 minutes at first and much less by February. For more independence skills, see our [JK readiness checklist](/blog/jk-readiness-checklist).

## Labelling and drying winter gear

Thirty sets of black snow pants and navy mitts look the same in a hallway.

- **Label every piece** with first and last name: both mitts, both boots, liners, hat, neck warmer, snow pants and coat.
- **Add a small picture symbol** inside for children who cannot read their name yet.
- **Bring wet gear home** each day if your school allows, or check that it is drying at school.
- **Dry boots and liners overnight** with liners removed. Stuffing boots with newspaper helps.
- **Check the spare clothes bag** each week in winter. Wet socks and pants come home often.

## Cold days, wind chill and frostbite

### When is it too cold for recess?

Each school or school board sets its own cold-weather rules for outdoor play, usually based on temperature and wind chill. The Canadian Paediatric Society suggests considering keeping children indoors when the temperature or wind chill is -27°C or colder. Environment and Climate Change Canada's [wind chill index](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/environment/weather/severeweather/wind-chill-index.html) rates -28 to -39 as high risk, when exposed skin can freeze in 10 to 30 minutes. Ask your school what threshold it uses.

### Signs of frostnip and frostbite

The Canadian Paediatric Society describes [frostnip as red, numb or tingly skin](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/safety-and-injury-prevention/frostbite), and more serious frostbite as skin that looks grey, white or shiny and has no feeling. If your child comes home with sore fingers or toes:

1. Remove wet clothing and dress them in dry, warm clothes.
2. Warm the area slowly with warm (not hot) water or skin-to-skin contact.
3. Do not rub or massage the skin.
4. Seek medical advice right away if skin stays white, waxy or numb.

### Storm days and buses

On freezing rain and heavy snow days, Ottawa school buses may be cancelled even when schools are open. Families using board transportation can check the [Ottawa Catholic School Board's inclement weather page](https://www.ocsb.ca/inclement-weather/), which points to the Ottawa Student Transportation Authority's app, website and local radio for cancellation notices. If you drive your child, leave extra time and clear snow off the car before the drop-off rush.

## Saving money on winter gear

Children outgrow snowsuits and boots every year or two, so new top-to-bottom gear each winter adds up. A few options:

- **Buy used** through local buy-and-sell groups and consignment stores, especially for snow pants and coats.
- **Hand down and swap** within your family, friends or mosque community.
- **Buy end-of-season** in late winter for next year, sizing up one size.
- **Spend most on boots and mitts,** since warm, dry hands and feet matter most.
