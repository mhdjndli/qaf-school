The best Arabic words for kids to learn first are the ones they can use every day: family members, body parts, food, things around the house and simple action words like "come", "sit" and "eat". Below are 100 essential Arabic words for kids, grouped into 10 themes of 10, each with full harakat (vowel marks), an easy pronunciation guide and the English meaning, plus a simple plan for teaching them at home.

> **Key takeaways**
> - Teach Arabic vocabulary in themes of 5–10 words, not as a random list. One theme per week or two is a good pace for ages 3–8.
> - Children learn words they hear in real moments. Say "مَاء" when you pour water, not only when you hold up a flashcard.
> - Every word below is in Modern Standard Arabic (fusha), the Arabic of books, school and, closely related, the Quran.
> - Harakat show exactly how to pronounce each word, so even parents who are still learning can model it correctly.
> - Many surah names are everyday words: the cow, the elephant, the sun, the moon. That makes a natural bridge to Quran learning.

## Why learn Arabic words by theme?

Arabic vocabulary sticks best when words are learned in small, connected groups. A child who learns "head, eyes, ears, nose" together can practise them all in one song or game, and each word helps them remember the others.

Themes also match how young children talk. They want to name what they see, what they eat and how they feel. Starting there gives them words they will actually use, which is what moves a word from "heard once" to "known".

There is a lovely reminder in the Quran that learning the names of things is a gift from Allah:

وَعَلَّمَ ءَادَمَ ٱلْأَسْمَآءَ كُلَّهَا

"And He taught Adam the names, all of them" ([Surah Al-Baqarah 2:31](https://quran.com/2/31), opening words).

## How to read the word tables

Each table gives the word with harakat, a simple transliteration and the meaning. A few pronunciation notes help:

- **ʿ** is the letter ع (ʿayn), a deep sound from the throat. **ʾ** is a short catch in the voice (hamza), like the break in "uh-oh".
- **ḥ** is ح, a breathy "h" from the throat. **kh** is خ, like the "ch" in Scottish "loch". **gh** is غ, like a gargled French "r".
- **ṣ, ḍ, ṭ, ẓ** are "heavy" versions of s, d, t and z. **q** is ق, made deeper in the throat than k.
- A doubled letter (like **umm** or **qiṭṭa**) has a shadda: hold the sound slightly longer.

Words are given in their pause form (how you say them on their own), without grammatical case endings. If you are new to the letters themselves, start with our step-by-step guide to [teaching the Arabic alphabet to kids](/blog/teach-arabic-alphabet-to-kids).

| Arabic | Say it | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| سَعِيد | saʿīd | happy |
| حَزِين | ḥazīn | sad |
| غَضْبَان | ghaḍbān | angry |

These are the masculine forms. For a girl, most take ـَة at the end: سَعِيدَة (saʿīda), حَزِينَة (ḥazīna), خَائِفَة (khāʾifa). Naming feelings in Arabic ("أَنْتَ حَزِين؟" Are you sad?) also helps children talk about emotions in any language.

## How to teach Arabic vocabulary to kids

### Step 1: Pick one theme and five words

Start small. Choose the theme your child cares about most (animals and food are usually winners) and pick five words. Add the other five once the first five come out of your child's mouth without help.

### Step 2: Use the words in real moments

A word heard while pouring water, putting on a coat or petting a cat is remembered far better than one seen on a card. Harvard's Center on the Developing Child describes this kind of back-and-forth talk as [serve and return](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/science/key-concepts/serve-and-return/): your child points or speaks, you respond, and those exchanges build language.

### Step 3: Mix Arabic into your normal sentences

You don't need to speak only Arabic. "Put on your قُبَّعَة, it's cold" works well for young children. Over time, swap more words into Arabic as your child's vocabulary grows.

### Step 4: Add pictures, objects and movement

Label things in the house, play "touch your رَأْس", sort toy animals, or hold up real fruit. Three- to six-year-olds learn through their hands and bodies as much as their ears. Our list of [Arabic learning games](/blog/arabic-games-for-kids) has ideas that need almost no preparation.

### Step 5: Review often, a little at a time

Two or three minutes of review every day beats a 30-minute session once a week. Quickly revisit old themes as you add new ones so words don't fade.

### Step 6: Let your child teach you

Ask your child to "teach" a grandparent, sibling or stuffed toy the words. Explaining a word is one of the best ways to remember it, and children love being the expert.

## A 10-week plan for 100 Arabic words

| Week | Theme | Easy daily moment to practise |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Family | Looking at family photos, video calls with relatives |
| 2 | Body parts | Bath time, wudu, tooth-brushing |
| 3 | Food and drink | Breakfast and snack time |
| 4 | Around the house | Tidy-up time, labelling furniture |
| 5 | Animals | Picture books, zoo or farm visits |
| 6 | Clothes | Getting dressed, packing a bag |
| 7 | Nature and weather | Looking out the window each morning |
| 8 | School and classroom | Homework and colouring time |
| 9 | Feelings and describing words | Bedtime chat about the day |
| 10 | Action words | All day long: "come", "sit", "wash" |

If a theme takes two weeks, that is fine. Steady progress matters more than finishing on schedule. Once these are solid, move on to [colours in Arabic](/blog/colors-in-arabic), [Arabic numbers](/blog/arabic-numbers-for-kids) and the days of the week.

## Common mistakes parents make

- **Teaching too many words at once.** A list of 50 words in a weekend rarely survives Monday.
- **Only testing, never using.** "What's this?" over and over feels like a quiz. Use the words naturally instead, and save questions for games.
- **Skipping the harakat.** Without vowel marks, a beginner can't tell how a word sounds. Keep them in until your child reads confidently.
- **Mixing dialect and fusha without noticing.** Both are valuable, but it helps to know which one you are teaching. Our guide to [fusha vs dialect for kids](/blog/fusha-vs-dialect-for-kids) explains the difference.
- **Stopping at nouns.** Action words and feelings let children make their first sentences, like "أَنَا جَائِع" (anā jāʾiʿ, I'm hungry).
