Yes, you can teach Arabic at home even if you don't speak Arabic yourself. Your job is not to be the teacher of grammar; it is to build an Arabic-rich routine: 15 minutes a day of songs, words, stories and short practice, plus a qualified teacher or class for structured learning. This guide shows you exactly how to do that, step by step, with vocabulary lists, a weekly plan and resources (including Ottawa options).

> **Key takeaways**
> - Consistency beats intensity: 15 minutes every day does more than two hours on a weekend.
> - Start with high-frequency words your child uses daily: greetings, family, food, colours and Islamic phrases.
> - Use native-speaker audio (songs, shows, apps) for pronunciation so you don't have to be the model.
> - Pair home practice with a real teacher or class. Home builds love and exposure; teachers build skills.
> - Expect understanding to come long before speaking, and celebrate small wins.

## Can you teach Arabic at home if you don't speak it?

You can. Many Muslim families in Canada speak Urdu, Somali, Bengali, French, English or another language at home, and still raise children who read Arabic and love it. What successful families tend to share is a routine, good audio models, and outside support.

Think of your role as three jobs:

1. **The environment builder.** You fill your home with Arabic sounds, words and books.
2. **The cheerleader.** You notice effort and celebrate progress.
3. **The connector.** You find teachers, classes and Arabic-speaking friends who can do what you can't.

You don't need fluency for any of these. You need about 15 minutes a day and a plan.

## Set realistic expectations first

Children learn a new language in stages. Many first go through a "silent period" where they understand much more than they say. This is normal and not a sign that home practice isn't working.

Every child is different, but here are reasonable goals for the first year of steady home practice plus a weekly class:

| Age | Reasonable first-year goals |
|---|---|
| 2–3 | Recognizes everyday words, joins in songs, responds to simple instructions |
| 4–5 | Knows greetings, colours, numbers to 10, Islamic phrases; begins letter sounds |
| 6–8 | Reads letters and simple vowelled words, understands short stories, uses short phrases |
| 9+ | Reads short texts, builds vocabulary quickly, can begin simple conversation |

**A note on fusha vs dialect.** Modern Standard Arabic (fusha) is the Arabic of books, school and media, and it is closest to the Arabic of the Quran. Dialects are spoken at home in different countries. If no one in your family speaks a dialect, focus on fusha. It is what children's books, apps and most classes use.

If your child is also learning English and French, our guide to [raising trilingual kids in Canada](/blog/raising-trilingual-kids-canada) explains how to balance three languages without overwhelming them.

## Step 1: Build a daily Arabic routine

The single most important thing you can do is attach Arabic to moments that already happen every day. Children thrive on predictable routines, and you won't have to remember to "do Arabic."

Good anchor moments:

- **Morning:** Greet your child with السَّلَامُ عَلَيْكُمْ and say بِسْمِ اللَّهِ before breakfast.
- **Car ride:** Play Arabic songs or nasheeds on the way to school.
- **After-school snack:** Name foods in Arabic. "مَاء or حَلِيب?" (water or milk?)
- **Bedtime:** Read or listen to one short Arabic story, then say a bedtime dua together. Our list of [daily duas for kids](/blog/daily-duas-for-kids) is a good starting point.

Start with just two anchors. Once they feel automatic, add a third.

## Step 2: Start with high-frequency vocabulary

Children learn words they hear and use every day. Pick one theme per week and use those words constantly, mixed into your normal English or home language: "Can you pass me the تُفَّاحَة?"

| Greetings | مِنْ فَضْلِكَ | min faḍlik | please |
| Family | أُمّ / أَب | umm / ab | mother / father |
| Family | أَخ / أُخْت | akh / ukht | brother / sister |
| Family | جَدّ / جَدَّة | jadd / jadda | grandfather / grandmother |
| Food | مَاء | māʾ | water |
| Food | خُبْز | khubz | bread |

### Islamic phrases to use every day

These phrases are a natural bridge between faith and language, and children pick them up quickly because they hear them in context:

- بِسْمِ اللَّهِ (bismillāh): in the name of Allah, before eating or starting something
- الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ (al-ḥamdu lillāh): praise be to Allah, after eating or when something good happens
- إِنْ شَاءَ اللَّهُ (in shāʾ Allāh): if Allah wills, when talking about the future
- مَا شَاءَ اللَّهُ (mā shāʾ Allāh): what Allah has willed, when admiring something
- جَزَاكَ اللَّهُ خَيْرًا (jazāk Allāhu khayran): may Allah reward you with good, as a heartfelt thank you

## Step 3: Label your home in Arabic

Stick simple labels on everyday objects: بَاب (door), نَافِذَة (window), كُرْسِيّ (chair), طَاوِلَة (table), سَرِير (bed). Write the Arabic word large, with harakat, and the transliteration small underneath for you.

Point and say the word each time you use the object. Swap in new labels every two or three weeks so they stay fresh. Children who see Arabic script every day become comfortable with it long before formal reading starts.

If your child is ready for letters, our guide on [how to teach the Arabic alphabet to kids](/blog/teach-arabic-alphabet-to-kids) gives a step-by-step method and a 28-letter chart you can print.

## Step 4: Use songs and shows with native speakers

Songs are the easiest way for non-Arabic-speaking parents to give children an accurate pronunciation model. Short, repetitive songs about letters, numbers, colours and animals are ideal. Learn the actions and sing along, even if your accent isn't perfect.

For shows, look for programs made for young children with clear speech:

- **Iftah Ya Simsim**, the Arabic version of Sesame Street, was relaunched in 2015 for children in the Arab world, and clips are available on its official YouTube channel.
- **Ahlan Simsim**, also from [Sesame Workshop](https://sesameworkshop.org/about-us/press-room/ahlan-simsim-friends-are-back-new-season-exciting-adventures-and-playful/), follows friends Basma and Jad, with episodes and extra content online.

Some Arabic children's shows are in fusha and others use a dialect, so preview a few episodes before choosing. For faith-based options, see our guide to the [best Islamic cartoons for kids](/blog/best-islamic-cartoons-for-kids).

**Keep screen time in balance.** The Canadian Paediatric Society's [Caring for Kids guidance](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/behavior-and-development/screen-time-and-young-children) recommends no screen use under age 2 (except video chats with family) and about an hour or less of routine screen time a day for ages 2 to 5. Watching together and talking about what you see makes that time far more valuable.

## Step 5: Read Arabic books together

Reading aloud builds vocabulary faster than almost anything else, and you can do it even before you read Arabic well.

- **Picture books with few words.** Point to pictures and name them in Arabic. The words matter less than the shared moment.
- **Bilingual books.** Read the English, then try the Arabic line with your child.
- **Books with audio.** Many Arabic children's books and reading apps include a recorded narrator, so your child hears correct pronunciation while you follow along.
- **The library.** Search the [Ottawa Public Library catalogue](https://biblioottawalibrary.ca/en) (or your local library) for Arabic-language children's books, and ask a librarian about holds from other branches.

## Step 6: Use apps as practice, not as the teacher

Good apps provide what a non-Arabic-speaking parent can't: native audio, instant feedback and endless patience. Use them for 10–15 minutes a day, ideally with you sitting nearby asking questions.

Choose apps that match your child's stage: playful vocabulary apps for toddlers, phonics apps for 4- to 6-year-olds, and levelled reading libraries for early readers. Free Read Along by Google even listens to children read Arabic aloud. Our comparison of the [best Arabic apps for kids](/blog/best-arabic-apps-for-kids) covers prices, ages and our honest take on each.

## Step 7: Find a teacher, class or school

Home practice builds exposure and love for the language. A trained teacher builds skills: correct pronunciation, reading, writing and grammar. Most families get the best results from both.

Your options usually include:

- **Weekend or after-school classes** at mosques, community centres or school boards
- **Online tutors** for one-on-one reading and conversation
- **Full-time Islamic schools** where Arabic is used daily
- **Arabic-speaking friends and family**, even one weekly playdate or video call with a grandparent

When comparing classes, ask: How many minutes of actual Arabic does my child hear? Is the teacher a fluent speaker? Is there homework you can support? Will my child get to speak, not just copy?

## Step 8: Learn alongside your child

Children are more motivated when they see a parent learning too. Even 10 minutes a day on a beginner app or course lets you read labels, follow songs and help with homework.

Make it a team effort. Let your child "teach" you a new word they learned in class. Researchers at the Harvard Center on the Developing Child describe these back-and-forth [serve-and-return interactions](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/science/key-concepts/serve-and-return/) as a foundation for how young children build skills, and language learning is no exception.

## A sample weekly plan

Here is a simple plan that fits into a busy family week, using about 15 minutes a day.

| Day | Activity (about 15 minutes) |
|---|---|
| Monday | Introduce 5 theme words with picture cards; add labels around the house |
| Tuesday | Arabic songs in the car; app practice after snack |
| Wednesday | Read a short Arabic picture book with audio |
| Thursday | Game: "I spy" or a scavenger hunt using the week's words |
| Friday | Islamic phrases and a short surah or dua together |
| Saturday | Class, tutor or video call with an Arabic-speaking relative |
| Sunday | Review: let your child "teach" you the week's words |

## Common challenges (and what helps)

**"My child answers in English."** That's normal. Repeat their answer back in Arabic without correcting them, and keep the conversation going.

**"We lost momentum."** Shrink the routine rather than stopping. Five minutes of songs keeps the habit alive until life calms down.

**"My child says Arabic is boring."** Change the format. Swap a worksheet for a cooking activity where you name ingredients, or a nature walk where you name colours.

**"I'm worried about my pronunciation."** Let native audio be the model, and focus on joy and consistency. Your effort is the example your child remembers.

## Start small, stay consistent

Teaching Arabic at home is not about perfect grammar or a perfect accent. It is about making Arabic a warm, everyday part of family life: a greeting in the morning, a song in the car, a story at bedtime. Add a good teacher, and your child has everything they need to grow.
