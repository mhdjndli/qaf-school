The best Arabic apps for kids depend on what your child needs right now: letters and sounds, early reading, or everyday vocabulary. For most families, a strong combination is one alphabet or phonics app (like Amal), one levelled reading library (like I Read Arabic or 3asafeer School), and free Read Along by Google for speaking practice. Below, we compare eight verified options by price, age, platform and the kind of Arabic they teach, with an honest take on each.

> **Key takeaways**
> - No single app teaches a child Arabic. Apps work best as 10–20 minutes of daily practice alongside a real human teacher or class.
> - For ages 3–6, choose apps focused on letter sounds, songs and play (Lamsa, Amal, Abjadiyat).
> - For readers aged 5 and up, levelled e-book libraries (I Read Arabic, 3asafeer School) give the most long-term value.
> - Duolingo's Arabic course is built for teens and adults, not young children.
> - Prices change often and vary by country, so always check the current App Store or Google Play price before subscribing.

## How to choose an Arabic app for your child

Before downloading anything, decide what job you want the app to do. An app that teaches the alphabet beautifully may have nothing to offer a child who already reads, and a reading library will frustrate a three-year-old.

Ask yourself four questions:

1. **What stage is my child at?** Pre-reader (ages 2–4), learning letters (4–6), early reader (5–8), or independent reader (8+).
2. **What do I want to strengthen?** Letter sounds, reading fluency, vocabulary, or listening and speaking.
3. **Can I support it?** If you don't read Arabic yourself, apps with native-speaker audio and read-aloud features matter much more.
4. **What will it cost over a year?** Many apps are "free" to download but lock most content behind a monthly subscription.

### Modern Standard Arabic vs dialect

Modern Standard Arabic (fusha) is the formal Arabic used in books, news, school and, closely related, the language of the Quran. Dialects (Levantine, Egyptian, Gulf, Maghrebi) are what families speak at home. Almost all children's reading apps use fusha, because that is the language of written Arabic.

If your goal is reading, school success or Quran-related literacy, fusha is the right choice. If grandparents speak a dialect, family conversation will build that side naturally. For more on why the early years are such a good window for this, see our article on [why early Arabic immersion matters](/blog/why-early-arabic-immersion-matters).

## The 8 best Arabic learning apps for kids, reviewed

Each review follows the same structure so you can compare quickly: what it is, who it's best for, price and ages, and our take.

### 1. Lamsa (Free download, subscription)

**What it is:** Lamsa is a bilingual Arabic–English early learning app with more than 1,200 games, videos, stories and activities covering letters, numbers, shapes, science and social-emotional skills. The [Lamsa App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/app/id517583488) labels it "Made for Ages 0–5" and includes parent progress reports.

**Best for:** Toddlers and preschoolers who need a gentle, playful introduction to Arabic sounds and words.

**Price and ages:** Free to download, with monthly and annual subscriptions for unlimited access (the premium plan allows up to five devices and offline downloads). Listed prices vary widely by region and promotion, so check the in-app price before you subscribe.

**Our take:** Lamsa is polished and engaging, and the bilingual design suits families where English is the stronger language. Because it covers many subjects, it is less focused on systematic Arabic reading than the apps below. Think of it as an "Arabic-rich play space" for ages 2–5 rather than a reading program.

### 2. I Read Arabic by Little Thinking Minds (Free download, subscription)

**What it is:** I Read Arabic is a digital levelled reading program from Little Thinking Minds with over 1,000 fiction and non-fiction e-books organized into reading levels, plus vocabulary lists, quizzes, games and voice recording. The publisher describes 19 levels designed for both native and non-native speakers from kindergarten to Grade 9.

**Best for:** Children aged 5 and up who know their letters and need lots of books at exactly the right level.

**Price and ages:** Free to download with a free trial. The [I Read Arabic App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1078563088) shows monthly, three-month and six-month subscriptions (the one-month plan is listed at US$5.99 at the time of writing).

**Our take:** Levelled reading is the single most valuable thing an app can offer a child learning to read Arabic, because it removes the guesswork of finding "not too hard, not too easy" books. The voice-recording feature lets parents who don't read Arabic still hear and celebrate progress. The same publisher also offers KG Arabic for younger learners.

### 3. 3asafeer School (Family subscription, 7-day free trial)

**What it is:** 3asafeer School is a library of levelled Arabic stories, cartoons, songs, games and worksheets. Books are levelled with the publisher's "Arabi21" system, which it describes as an Arabic equivalent to Lexile levels.

**Best for:** Families with two or more children, and kids who love stories.

**Price and ages:** According to the [3asafeer subscription page](https://3asafeer.com/en/subscribe), the family plan covers up to five readers and costs US$9.99 per month, or US$6.99 per month billed annually, with a 7-day free trial. It is designed for ages 3 to 18 and works on the web as well as iOS and Android.

**Our take:** The family plan is excellent value when siblings share it, and the story library is large and varied. As with any big library, preview stories for younger children. You know your family's values best, and a quick read-through takes a minute.

### 4. Amal: Learn Arabic for Kids (Free lessons, subscription)

**What it is:** Amal is a phonics-first app that teaches Arabic letters by shape and sound, then builds to first words, simple sentences and early reading through short daily lessons.

**Best for:** Ages 4–7 who are learning the alphabet, especially in homes where parents are not confident Arabic readers.

**Price and ages:** Free to download with a limited set of free lessons; a subscription unlocks all levels. The App Store listing describes it as designed for ages 3–12.

**Our take:** Amal follows the order that works in classrooms: sounds first, then blending, then reading. It is a strong first app for a 4- or 5-year-old. Pair it with hands-on letter play, which we explain in our guide on [how to teach the Arabic alphabet to kids](/blog/teach-arabic-alphabet-to-kids).

### 5. Abjadiyat (Free download, paid content)

**What it is:** Abjadiyat is an Arabic learning app from Alef Education for children aged 3 to 8. Lessons use songs, videos and interactive activities aligned with Arab school curricula, and teachers can assign content and track quiz results.

**Best for:** Children whose school or teacher already uses Abjadiyat, or families who want a structured, curriculum-style sequence.

**Price and ages:** Free to download for ages 3–8, with full content unlocked through a purchase or school access. Check the current pricing in the app.

**Our take:** Abjadiyat's strength is structure: it feels like a classroom sequence rather than a random collection of games. It was designed with Arabic-speaking children in mind, so non-Arabic-speaking families may need to sit alongside their child at first.

### 6. Read Along by Google (Free)

**What it is:** Read Along is Google's free reading app with an in-app reading buddy that listens as children read aloud and gives real-time feedback using speech recognition. Google added Arabic stories in 2020.

**Best for:** Early readers (roughly ages 5–9) who can decode simple words and need fluency practice.

**Price and ages:** Completely free, with no subscription. According to the [official Read Along site](https://readalong.google/), it is available as an Android app that works offline and as a web experience on any device.

**Our take:** Read Along is the best free option on this list and a great "second app" alongside a paid reading library. The listening feedback is especially helpful for parents who can't correct pronunciation themselves. There is no native iPhone app, so iOS families use it in a web browser.

### 7. Gus on the Go: Arabic ($3.99 one-time)

**What it is:** Gus on the Go is a vocabulary app in which a cartoon owl introduces nearly 90 Arabic words in themes like animals, food and clothing, with native-speaker audio, review games and a trophy room.

**Best for:** Preschoolers learning their very first Arabic words.

**Price and ages:** A one-time purchase of US$3.99 on the [Gus on the Go: Arabic App Store page](https://apps.apple.com/app/id836566633), with no subscription. It is listed as made for ages 0–5.

**Our take:** Simple, cheerful, and paid once, which many parents appreciate. However, the app has not been updated since 2018, so check that it runs well on your device. It teaches words only, not reading.

### 8. Duolingo Arabic course (Free, optional paid plan)

**What it is:** Duolingo's Arabic course teaches Modern Standard Arabic script, sounds and basic vocabulary in short gamified lessons for English speakers.

**Best for:** Older children (around 10+) learning with a parent, and parents who want to learn alongside their kids.

**Price and ages:** Free with ads; a paid subscription removes ads and adds features. Duolingo's main app is designed for teens and adults, and its account terms include age requirements, so younger children should use it only through a parent's account. Duolingo's apps for young children (such as Duolingo ABC) do not teach Arabic.

**Our take:** Duolingo is not an app for preschoolers, and its Arabic course is smaller than its Spanish or French courses. It shines as a parent tool: if you're learning to read Arabic yourself, it helps you support your child at home.

## Best Arabic apps by age

If you only want a quick recommendation, start here.

- **Ages 2–3:** Lamsa or Gus on the Go, used together with a parent, in very short sessions. The [Canadian Paediatric Society's Caring for Kids guidance on screen use](https://caringforkids.cps.ca/handouts/behavior-and-development/screen-time-and-young-children) recommends no screen use under age 2 (except video chatting with family) and about an hour or less of routine screen time per day for ages 2 to 5.
- **Ages 4–5:** Amal or Abjadiyat for letters and sounds, plus Arabic songs and read-alouds off-screen.
- **Ages 5–7:** Amal to finish the alphabet, then I Read Arabic or 3asafeer for levelled reading, plus Read Along for reading aloud.
- **Ages 8–10:** A levelled library for daily reading; Duolingo if the child wants to learn with a parent.

## Free Arabic apps for kids

Families on a budget can build a solid routine without paying anything:

- **Read Along by Google** is fully free.
- **Amal, Lamsa and I Read Arabic** each offer free lessons or free trials, which are enough to judge whether your child enjoys them.
- **Duolingo** is free with ads for older learners and parents.

A smart approach is to use free trials one at a time, for a week each, and only subscribe to the one your child asks to open.

## How to get real results from Arabic apps

Apps are tools, not teachers. In our classroom we've found that children learn letters fastest when they **say, see, touch and write** them, and a screen only covers the first two.

These habits make the biggest difference:

1. **Keep sessions short.** Ten to fifteen minutes of focused practice beats an hour of tapping.
2. **Sit with your child when you can.** Ask, "What sound does that letter make?" Co-viewing turns passive screen time into conversation.
3. **Take it off the screen.** After a lesson on the letter ب, hunt for things that start with "b" in Arabic: بَاب (door), بَطَّة (duck).
4. **Use apps at a consistent time,** such as after school snack, rather than as a reward or a way to calm down.
5. **Set up the device first.** Turn off in-app purchases and lock the app. Our guide to [parental controls for Muslim families](/blog/parental-controls-muslim-families) walks through each step.

## Apps vs Arabic classes: what apps can't do

Apps are good at repetition, pronunciation models and motivation. They are weak at conversation, correcting handwriting, and responding to a child's questions in Arabic. Children become comfortable speakers by hearing and using a language with real people, every day.
